# How languages spread: modern stops (about 1400 CE to today)

Research notes for the "How languages spread" page. Scope is colonial and modern spread plus today's picture. Researched 2026-10-17. Citation keys like [ETH-29] point to the Bibliography.

Verification levels used in the Bibliography: FETCHED (page text read directly), SEARCH-CONFIRMED (a search result quoted the passage, but the page itself could not be opened), SECONDHAND (the number reaches us only through a third party). Ethnologue's website, Britannica and several others return HTTP 403 to automated fetches, so every Ethnologue figure below was first SEARCH-CONFIRMED or SECONDHAND. Re-check them in a browser before publishing.

Second check, 2026-10-29: several sources were re-read, some through Internet Archive captures (the entry links the capture that was read). Where that changed a claim or a label, the entry says so. Ethnologue's 29th-edition post and its "How many languages" page are now FETCHED from captures; the 2026 speaker table is still SECONDHAND.

Conventions: L1 = first-language (native) speakers. L2 = additional-language speakers. "Total" = L1 + L2. Years are CE. Coordinates are decimal degrees (north and east positive).

## Spanish: from Castile across the Americas

- id: `spanish-to-the-americas`
- kind: language
- date range (plain text): from 1492, with the main spread over the 1500s
- representative year: 1492
- source location: Castile, central Spain. Toledo, 39.86, -4.03
- location uncertainty: Castile is a region, not a point. Most ships left from Seville and Cadiz in the south, so Seville (37.39, -5.99) is the other defensible pin. Pin on Castile and let the arrows start at the coast if you want the sailing story.
- spread to (approximate):
  - Santo Domingo, Hispaniola: 18.47, -69.89
  - Mexico City: 19.43, -99.13
  - Lima: -12.05, -77.04
  - Buenos Aires: -34.60, -58.38
- uncertainty on destinations: these are modern city centres standing in for whole regions. Spanish reached the Americas in waves, first the Caribbean, then Mexico and Peru, then the south. No single place is "where it landed".

Claims:

1. Columbus reached the Bahamian island of Guanahani (San Salvador) in 1492 and took possession of the islands "for their Highnesses", the Spanish monarchs. The exact island is debated (San Salvador or Samana Cay). [LOC-TAINO] [LOC-LANDING]
2. Today the Instituto Cervantes counts about 520 million native speakers of Spanish (2025 yearbook, L1), the third-largest native-speaker community after Mandarin and Hindi. It counts about 635 million "potential" speakers when people with limited competence (about 92 million) and learners (over 24 million) are added. [CERV-25-EFE] [CERV-25-ES]
3. Mexico has more Spanish speakers than any other country: about 125 million of 485 million native speakers, according to a UNAM linguist quoted in Gaceta UNAM (24 April 2025). [GACETA-UNAM]
4. Dropped in the second check: "Mexico alone holds about a quarter of all native speakers", "more than 80 percent in the Americas" (both from El Orden Mundial only, [EOM-ES]) and the 2020 census total as a ranking source (the INEGI file was a Jalisco state bulletin, [INEGI-2020]). Gaceta UNAM's 125 of 485 million is roughly a quarter, but use its numbers, not the El Orden Mundial wording.

Disagreements and caveats:

- Native speakers: Cervantes 2025 says 520 million (one news report of the same launch quotes "more than 530 million"). Ethnologue's 2026 edition lists about 487 million L1 and about 561 million total, from a secondhand table [ETH-29-TABLE] that must not be cited. Ethnologue's 2025 edition listed about 558 million total [STATISTA-25-ETH]. The gap comes from different counting methods. Always name the source and say "about".
- Rank by native speakers: the sources disagree, and this note does not resolve it. Gaceta UNAM says Spanish is second, after Mandarin, with 485 million native speakers [GACETA-UNAM]. Cervantes 2025 says 520 million and third, behind Mandarin and Hindi [CERV-25-ES]. Ethnologue's "most spoken language" page says Spanish is 2nd by native speakers [ETH-FAQ-L1]. Name the source with the rank.
- Mexico's size: Statista's 2025 figure for native Spanish speakers in Mexico is about 138 million [STATISTA-ES-25]. That is higher than Mexico's total population in the 2020 census (126 million, per a state bulletin [INEGI-2020]). Treat 138 million as unreliable and use Gaceta UNAM's "about 125 million".
- Claim 3 rests on one university news article quoting one researcher; it names no data source. The Cervantes yearbook's own country table was not opened.
- Not covered: how Spanish displaced or mixed with Indigenous languages region by region.

Card description (draft): "From 1492, ships from Spain carried Castilian Spanish to the Caribbean, then Mexico and South America. Today about 500 million people speak it as a first language, and Mexico has the most speakers of any country."

## Portuguese: to Brazil and Africa

- id: `portuguese-to-brazil-and-africa`
- kind: language
- date range: from 1500 in Brazil; Africa from the 1500s on
- representative year: 1500
- source location: Lisbon, Portugal. 38.72, -9.14
- location uncertainty: Lisbon is a good pin for the Portuguese crown and its ships. The language itself grew in the north-west of the Iberian Peninsula.
- spread to (approximate):
  - Porto Seguro, Bahia, Brazil: -16.44, -39.06 (the 1500 landfall)
  - Salvador, Brazil: -12.97, -38.50
  - Luanda, Angola: -8.84, 13.23
  - Mozambique coast, near Ilha de Moçambique: -15.04, 40.73
- uncertainty on destinations: Porto Seguro is the standard landfall point but some Brazilian local historians argue for sites further north. Pins for Angola and Mozambique are coastal stand-ins; Portuguese there spread inland over centuries.

Claims:

1. A fleet of 13 ships under Pedro Alvares Cabral left Lisbon on 9 March 1500 and reached the coast of what is now Brazil in late April, anchoring at Porto Seguro, in what is now Bahia. Fausto gives 21 April for the sighting and the next day for the anchorage; other accounts give 22 April for the sighting of Monte Pascoal (see caveats). Historians still debate whether the landfall was accidental. [FAUSTO-2014] [RAMOS-2023] [ARQ-NAC]
2. Brazil is the largest Portuguese-speaking country. Its 2022 census counted 203,080,756 people (IBGE's revised figure of 31 August 2023; the first release on 28 June 2023 gave 203,062,512). [IBGE-REV-2023] [IBGE-2022]
3. The Instituto Camoes estimated that 260 million people (about 3.7 percent of world population) speak Portuguese, as reported in 2022, and Agência Brasil calls it the fourth most spoken mother tongue, after Mandarin, English and Spanish. Portuguese is official in nine countries and in Macau. [AGBR-2022]
4. Portuguese is a first language for far fewer people in Mozambique than in Angola. In Mozambique's 2017 census about 47 percent of people aged 5 and over speak Portuguese, and about 16.5 percent have it as a first language. In Angola's 2014 census about 71 percent say they use Portuguese at home. [RILP-270] [ANG-2014-PRESS]

Disagreements and caveats:

- Speakers: Camoes says 260 million (2022). Agência Brasil's article does not say whether that counts first-language speakers only, and the same article ranks Portuguese by mother-tongue speakers, so do not gloss 260 million as "all speakers" or as "native speakers". Ethnologue's 2026 table, seen only secondhand and not citable, lists about 252 million L1 and 269 million total [ETH-29-TABLE]. Camoes's method is not published in what we could open.
- Mozambique 2017: one article gives 47.4 percent speaking Portuguese; a table attributed to the national statistics office (via Timbane and Andrade 2019) gives 16.5 percent L1 and 60.3 percent L2; another paper says just under 20 percent L1. The numbers do not reconcile, so the card should avoid Mozambique percentages. [RILP-270] [SEARCH-MOZ]
- Angola 2014: 71 percent is "language used at home", not strictly first language (the census did not separate L1 from L2). The figure comes from press and academic summaries of the national statistics institute's census, not the institute's own report. WEAK for exact value. [ANG-2014-PRESS]
- Claim 1, dates: Fausto's excerpt was read on 2026-10-29 (the PDF's text was extracted directly). It says the fleet "sighted what would become Brazilian land" on 21 April, a landing party went briefly ashore that day, and "on the following day the fleet anchored at Porto Seguro". Other accounts give 22 April for the first sighting: a 2023 Revista USP review of an edition of Caminha's letter says land was sighted on 22 April and named Monte Pascoal [RAMOS-2023], and the first research pass reported Brazilian federal and Arquivo Nacional pages giving 22 April (both blocked automated reading on 2026-10-29). The commonly repeated anchorage at Porto Seguro on about 24 April was not found in any source opened here. Say "late April 1500" and avoid exact days unless a source is named.
- Brazil's population: the gov.br release of the first results gives only "203,1 milhões" [IBGE-2022]. The exact first-release and revised totals come from press reports of IBGE's releases [IBGE-REV-2023]. IBGE's own release page sat behind a bot check.
- Dropped: Luanda's founding in 1576 by Paulo Dias de Novais. Only Wikipedia-derived pages supported it. Mozambique's first Portuguese contact (1498 or 1500s) was not sourced. Both are left out of claims; Luanda and Mozambique pins are for map direction only.

Card description (draft): "In 1500 a Portuguese fleet reached the coast of Brazil, and the language later took root in Angola and Mozambique too. Brazil has by far the most Portuguese speakers, about 200 million people in all."

## Russian: eastward across Siberia

- id: `russian-across-siberia`
- kind: language
- date range: about 1580s to 1640s across Siberia; into Central Asia mainly in the 1860s
- representative year: 1582
- source location: Moscow, 55.75, 37.62
- location uncertainty: Moscow is the political centre. The expeditions started in the Urals (the Stroganov lands) and used northern river routes.
- spread to (approximate):
  - Tobolsk, western Siberia: 58.20, 68.25 (founded 1587)
  - Okhotsk, Pacific coast: 59.36, 143.24 (founded 1647)
  - Tashkent, Central Asia: 41.30, 69.24 (fell to Russia 1865)
- uncertainty on destinations: Tobolsk and Okhotsk are real founding sites. Tashkent is one city in a long, uneven advance into Central Asia.

Claims:

1. Bassin: "Most accounts date Yermak's initial crossing of the Urals to 1582, and the Pacific coast was reached by Ivan Moskvitin in 1639." The movement began with Yermak's cossack band crossing the Urals and defeating the Tatar prince Kuchum, pulled east by the fur trade. [BASSIN-1999]
2. Secondary details, from EBSCO only (WEAK): Yermak led about 850 Cossacks; Tobolsk was founded in 1587 and Okhotsk in 1647. [EBSCO-RUS-EXPL]
3. In Central Asia, many historians date the start of the Russian conquest of Turkestan to the fall of Tashkent in 1865. The Turkestan Governor-Generalship was set up in 1867. [MORRISON-2021] [SEARCH-TURK]
4. Russian is used by about 210 to 260 million people in total, depending on the edition and method (see below). Its numbers include many second-language speakers in former Soviet countries. [ETH-29-TABLE] [LINGODA]

Disagreements and caveats:

- Dates: Yermak's campaign start is given as 1581 or 1582 and his death as 1584 or 1585 depending on source. Bassin says "most accounts" give 1582 for the crossing; "1582 to 1585" as a campaign span was EBSCO's and is no longer used. Use "1580s" or "about 1582".
- Speaker counts disagree widely. A 2019 Ethnologue edition (via a blog) gives about 258 million total with about 154 million L1 [LINGODA]. Ethnologue's 2026 table (secondhand) gives about 210 million total with about 133 million L1 [ETH-29-TABLE]. Treat any number as WEAK; the card avoids a figure.
- Claim 1 now rests on Bassin 1999 (Cambridge University Press), whose chapter summary was read on 2026-10-29; it replaces EBSCO, as on the retired page. Dartmouth's Encyclopedia Arctica also gives 1639 for the Pacific (see README). The 850 Cossacks and the Tobolsk and Okhotsk founding dates still rest on EBSCO alone (plus Wikipedia-derived search results, which we do not cite). WEAK.
- Claim 3 (Morrison, Cambridge UP) is SEARCH-CONFIRMED only. The 1867 Governor-Generalship date came from Wikipedia-derived search summaries and is WEAK.
- Dropped: Russian-speaking share of Kazakhstan's 2021 census (reported as about 84 percent only via Wikipedia; the statistics bureau's own table could not be opened). The Russian 2021 census language results are also described as unreliable by a Russian sociolinguistics paper. [SOCIOLING-RU]

Card description (draft): "In the 1580s Russian Cossacks crossed the Ural Mountains, and within about sixty years Russian speakers had reached the Pacific. In the 1800s the language also spread into Central Asia."

## English: from England to the world

- id: `english-worldwide`
- kind: language
- date range: from 1607 in North America, 1788 in Australia, and across India and Africa through the 1700s to 1900s; global influence in the 1900s
- representative year: 1607
- source location: London, England. 51.51, -0.13
- location uncertainty: English grew across England, not in London alone. London stands in for the crown, the trading companies and the ports.
- spread to (approximate):
  - Jamestown, Virginia: 37.21, -76.78
  - Sydney Cove, Australia: -33.86, 151.21
  - Kolkata (Calcutta), India: 22.57, 88.36
  - Lagos, Nigeria: 6.52, 3.38
- uncertainty on destinations: Jamestown and Sydney Cove are documented settlement sites. Kolkata and Lagos are illustrative for South Asia and West Africa; neither is backed by a dated claim here, and English reached those regions at different times and in different ways.

Claims:

1. Jamestown, Virginia, founded in 1607 by 104 English men and boys, was the first permanent English settlement in North America. [NPS-JAMESTOWN]
2. The First Fleet of 11 ships under Captain Arthur Phillip set up a convict settlement at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788, after first reaching Botany Bay on 18 January. [NMA-FF]
3. English was the world's most-spoken language by total speakers in Ethnologue's 2025 edition: about 1.5 billion (about 1,528 million) people, of whom only about 390 million speak it as a first language. In the 2026 edition the total is about 1.49 billion. [STATISTA-25-ETH] [VC-2025] [STATISTA-2026]
4. A separate British Council estimate (2013) put English speakers at "a useful level" at about 1.75 billion, one in four people. [BC-ENGLISH-EFFECT]

Disagreements and caveats:

- Totals: Ethnologue 2025 gives about 1.53 billion; Ethnologue 2026 about 1.49 billion (Statista 2026 page, and a secondhand table with L1 372 million and L2 1,121 million [ETH-29-TABLE]); British Council 2013 gives 1.75 billion. These are different definitions and years. Use "about 1.5 billion" and say "including people who speak it as an extra language".
- L1 vs total: if you count only first-language speakers, English is third behind Mandarin and Spanish (Ethnologue FAQ). [ETH-FAQ-L1]
- Claims 3 and 4: the Ethnologue pages themselves were blocked. The 2025 numbers come via Statista and Visual Capitalist as relayed by search results (SEARCH-CONFIRMED); the British Council headline is from its report summary (SEARCH-CONFIRMED; its PDF timed out).
- Claim 2: the National Museum of Australia pages returned 403 to fetching; the details come from search summaries of its "Defining Moments" pages. SEARCH-CONFIRMED.
- Dropped: India's English speakers. Census of India 2011 is reported as about 129 million total (2.6 lakh L1), but only from secondary sites and a Wikipedia table; the census table itself was not opened. WEAK, not used.
- Dropped: the East India Company charter (31 December 1600, The National Archives) and Macaulay's 1835 Minute. The charter date is SEARCH-CONFIRMED but we have no dated source for English-in-India itself; the Minute text was not found.
- No source was found for English spreading to West or East Africa; add one if the page wants an Africa claim.

Card description (draft): "English travelled with settlers, traders and soldiers, from Jamestown in 1607 to Sydney in 1788 and across the British Empire. Counting people who speak it as an extra language, about 1.5 billion people use it, more than any other language."

## French: Canada, the Caribbean and Africa

- id: `french-in-canada-and-africa`
- kind: language
- date range: Quebec from 1608; Caribbean colonies from the 1600s; West and Central Africa mainly from the late 1800s
- representative year: 1608
- source location: Paris, France. 48.86, 2.35
- location uncertainty: Paris was the capital and court. Settlers came from western France, especially ports like La Rochelle, but we have no source in this file for that.
- spread to (approximate):
  - Quebec City: 46.81, -71.21
  - Cap-Haitien (former Cap-Francais), Haiti: 19.76, -72.20
  - Dakar, Senegal: 14.72, -17.47
  - Kinshasa, DR Congo: -4.44, 15.27
- uncertainty on destinations: Quebec City is the founding site. Cap-Haitien stands in for the colony of Saint-Domingue. Dakar and Kinshasa stand in for French West and Central Africa; neither city is a colonial founding point in this file.

Claims:

1. Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec City in 1608, arriving at the site on 3 July. [CAN-ENC-CHAMPLAIN]
2. In Canada's 2021 census, 19.6 percent of the population (about 7.2 million people) had French as a mother tongue. In Quebec the share was 74.8 percent. [STATCAN-2021]
3. The French government in West Africa was formed in 1895 (the Government-General of French West Africa) and French Equatorial Africa covered Gabon, Middle Congo, Ubangi-Shari and Chad. [LOC-FWA] [LOC-FEA]
4. The Organisation internationale de la Francophonie (OIF) counted about 321 million French speakers in 2022 and about 396 million in its March 2026 report, about 65 percent of them in Africa. The same report lists "près de 170 millions d'apprenants" (nearly 170 million learners) as a separate figure; it does not say they are part of the 396 million. The 2026 jump comes partly from a new method that counts children aged 6 to 9 who read French. [OIF-2022] [OIF-2026] [ESSENTIEL-ECO]

Disagreements and caveats:

- OIF's own counts and Ethnologue's differ a lot. Ethnologue 2026 (secondhand) lists about 334 million total with 75 million L1 and 258 million L2 [ETH-29-TABLE]; OIF 2026 says 396 million and ranks French 4th, while Ethnologue puts French 5th or 6th, behind Standard Arabic. Say "about 300 to 400 million, depending on who counts" or name OIF explicitly.
- OIF's 2022 share in Africa is 62 percent in one release and "about 60 percent" in others. The 2026 report gives 65 percent.
- DRC: one article reports the OIF 2026 report as giving about 67.8 million French speakers in the DRC, more than metropolitan France [ESSENTIEL-ECO]. The OIF page itself does not mention the DRC. WEAK.
- Dropped: "Kinshasa is the largest French-speaking city". It is widely repeated (Le Figaro/Paris Zigzag cite 12.8 million for Kinshasa against 11 million for Paris; Afrik.com and Le Courrier de Kinshasa repeat it), but the sources are press articles with changing numbers, and one source puts French use in Kinshasa at only 36 percent while another cites 92 percent from a 2010 survey. We could not find the OIF or Observatoire stating it directly. Do not use it on the page.
- Claims 1 and 3: the Canadian Encyclopedia body text and the Library of Congress pages did not load; both are SEARCH-CONFIRMED. The Quebec-founding line is consistent in several search results.
- Not sourced: French in the Caribbean (Saint-Domingue's start), and Belgian Congo's role in making French the DRC's official language. Add a source before writing about them.

Card description (draft): "French settled Quebec in 1608 and later spread across the Caribbean and, in the late 1800s, much of West and Central Africa. Today about 300 to 400 million people speak it, and roughly two in three live in Africa."

## Creoles: languages born from colonial contact

- id: `colonial-creoles`
- kind: creole
- date range: about 1600s to 1800s
- representative year: 1700 (Haitian Creole is described as established by the early 1700s)
- source location: Saint-Domingue (now Haiti), around Cap-Haitien. 19.76, -72.20
- location uncertainty: the stop covers three separate languages on three continents. Haiti is the pin; the others are listed as "spread to" for display only. The origin of Nigerian Pidgin is the least certain (see caveats).
- spread to (approximate, as other examples, not as "spread from Haiti"):
  - Rabaul, New Britain, Papua New Guinea (Tok Pisin): -4.20, 152.17
  - Calabar, south-east Nigeria (Nigerian Pidgin): 4.95, 8.33
- uncertainty on destinations: Rabaul stands in for the plantation areas of the Bismarck Archipelago. Calabar is the place of the oldest written evidence of Nigerian Pidgin (Antera Duke's diary). The atlas site APiCS pins Nigerian Pidgin at 6.36, 5.56, an unlabelled point further west.

Claims:

1. Haitian Creole grew out of contact between French and the many languages of enslaved Africans in colonial Saint-Domingue. It was a distinct language by the early 1700s. It has over 10 million speakers and is an official language of Haiti alongside French (1987 constitution). [EBSCO-HAITIAN]
2. Tok Pisin grew from Pacific Pidgin English. A Melanesian form began to take shape from 1863 when Pacific labour trade sent workers to plantations in Queensland and later Samoa. Returned workers spread it through the Bismarck Archipelago and German New Guinea. About 3 to 5 million people speak it, mostly as an additional language, and it is one of Papua New Guinea's three national languages. [APICS-TOKPISIN]
3. Nigerian Pidgin is mostly an additional language. Ethnologue's 2026 table (secondhand) lists about 5 million L1 and about 116 million L2 speakers, about 121 million in total. A journal estimate puts L2 users at over 75 million and L1 at 3 to 5 million. [ETH-29-TABLE] [NIGERIAN-PIDGIN-SEARCH]
4. A comparative atlas of 76 pidgin and creole languages exists (APiCS, Oxford University Press, 2013), edited by Michaelis, Maurer, Haspelmath and Huber. [APICS]

Disagreements and caveats:

- Haitian Creole speakers: "over 10 to 12 million" and "over 13 million" both appear in the same EBSCO article. Use "more than 10 million".
- Haiti's legal status: the EBSCO article says Creole became official in 1987, but elsewhere the same article says the 1987 constitution recognised both languages as national. Safe wording: "official alongside French".
- Nigerian Pidgin: the L2 count ranges from over 75 million to 116 million. Origins are disputed, from contact on the Calabar coast in the 1700s to arguments (Faraclas) for earlier trade pidgins. WEAK; the card says only that it is widely used as a shared language.
- Tok Pisin: other sources give 130,000 L1 and about 4 million L2, or up to 500,000 L1; the APiCS figure is used above.
- Claims 1 and 4 are SEARCH-CONFIRMED or EBSCO (secondary). Claim 3's Ethnologue number is SECONDHAND.

Card description (draft): "When people with no shared language were brought together by trade, plantations and slavery, new languages grew. Haitian Creole, Tok Pisin and Nigerian Pidgin each began this way and are now spoken by millions."

## The world's languages today

- id: `world-languages-today`
- kind: today
- date range: 2026
- representative year: 2026
- location: no single place. Use a global marker, for example a centred or spread-out set of pins. Suggested anchor if one point is required: 20.0, 0.0 (open map centre).
- location uncertainty: this stop is about the whole world; any single coordinate is only a layout choice.

Claims:

1. Ethnologue's 29th edition (announced 21 February 2026) lists 7,170 living languages, "a net increase of 11" since the 28th edition, which gave 7,159. Ethnologue says the total "cannot be known precisely". [ETH-29] [ETH-HOWMANY]; corroborated by [OWID-ETL]
2. Glottolog, which is a separate catalogue, counts 7,674 spoken first languages (plus sign languages and others, 8,238 entries in all) grouped into 246 families and 183 isolates. [GLOTTOLOG]
3. By total speakers (L1 plus L2), Ethnologue ranks English first, then Mandarin Chinese, Hindi and Spanish. By first-language speakers Mandarin is first, then Spanish, English and Hindi. [ETH-FAQ-L1] [ETH-200]
4. Ethnologue says the 20 largest languages are the first language of more than 3.7 billion people, nearly half of humanity, while those 20 are only about 0.3 percent of all languages (28th-edition page). [ETH-HOWMANY]

Ranking numbers (Ethnologue 2026, millions). NOT CITABLE: this table was copied from a Wikipedia page that reproduces Ethnologue's figures [ETH-29-TABLE]; nobody has seen it on ethnologue.com. Use it as a lead only. Every 2026 L1, L2 or total figure in these notes rests on it, including Spanish 487 million L1 and 561 million total, English 1,493 million total (372 L1, 1,121 L2), Portuguese 252/269, French 75/258/334, Russian 133/210 and Nigerian Pidgin 5/116/121.

Disagreements and caveats:

- Mandarin is "Mandarin Chinese" in Ethnologue's list. Counting all Chinese varieties gives larger numbers; older Statista charts gave 1.31 billion for Chinese overall and 898 million for Mandarin L1. Use the edition and say "Mandarin Chinese".
- The 2025 and 2026 editions differ by up to about 40 million for English and about 12 million for Hindi. Say "about" and name the edition year.
- The brief asked about "about half of people speak one of about 20 languages". The Ethnologue wording is about first-language speakers: more than 3.7 billion of the world's people, which is nearly half. Earlier (2022) Ethnologue said 23 languages account for more than half of the world's population. [ETH-TWEET-2022] These cutoffs differ, so quote one.
- The ranking of French versus Standard Arabic differs across Ethnologue pages and editions; the 2025 and 2026 numbers put Arabic ahead. Treat "top ten" lists as approximate.
- Glottolog's counts are higher than the brief expected (7,674 against about 7,000). It could not be fetched directly (connection refused); the numbers come from the search result's quote of its information page, dated 2026-03-02, with no version named. Whether "246 families" includes the 183 isolates was not clear from the quote. Label as SEARCH-CONFIRMED.
- Voronoi, citing Ethnologue and Derivation, reports that the top ten languages by first-language speakers reached just over 3.1 billion people in 2025, about 38 percent of the world, down from about 44 percent in 1996. [VORONOI]

Card description (draft): "There are about 7,200 living languages in the world. The 20 biggest are the first language of nearly half of all people, while the rest are spoken by far fewer."

## Languages lost and at risk

- id: `languages-lost-and-at-risk`
- kind: today
- date range: past 500 years to today
- representative year: 2026
- location: global; where a single pin is needed, use Australia as the clearest documented example. Central Australia: -25.0, 133.0
- location uncertainty: the Australian pin is a map layout choice, not the location of any one language.

Claims:

1. Ethnologue says roughly 44 percent of the world's languages are now endangered, often with fewer than 1,000 users left. [ETH-HOWMANY]
2. Ethnologue's FAQ lists 1,431 languages with fewer than 1,000 first-language speakers (about one in five), 463 with fewer than 100, and 110 with fewer than 10. [ETH-FAQ-SMALL]
3. UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger (3rd edition, 2010) counts about 2,500 endangered languages, a figure it describes as the estimate generally accepted by linguists. [UNESCO-ATLAS]
4. Researchers analysing 6,511 spoken languages (Bromham et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2022) warn that without action language loss could triple in 40 years and more than 1,500 languages could go silent by 2100. [BROMHAM-2022] [ANU-2021]
5. On colonisation: co-author Felicity Meakins said languages "have been silenced as the result of brutal colonial policies designed to suppress Indigenous languages". In Australia more than 250 First Nations languages were spoken before colonisation; the researchers say about 40 are still in use and only 12 are being passed to children. [UQ-2021] [ANU-2021]

Disagreements and caveats:

- The share endangered varies. Ethnologue's "How many languages" page says "Roughly 44% of all languages are now endangered". The Internet Archive capture of 2026-01-09 shows that page with "7,159 languages are in use today" and a 28th-edition (2025) citation, so the 44 percent belongs to the 28th edition [ETH-HOWMANY]; whether the 29th edition changed it was not seen. Earlier Ethnologue figures were 3,045 endangered of about 7,164 (about 42 percent, 27th edition); blogs quote 3,193 and 3,226. Say "about 4 in 10". UNESCO's 2,500 is a different method and an older date.
- The brief's "about 40 percent" matches Ethnologue's 42 to 44 percent only roughly. "Around 4 in 10" is safe.
- Honest framing of causes: the Bromham paper's statistical results link endangerment to road density and years of schooling, and it finds that contact with other languages is not itself a driver of loss. The colonial link comes from the authors' public statements about Australia [UQ-2021], not from a global result in the paper. Do not say the paper proves colonisation caused global loss.
- Australia counts differ: the Bromham team says about 40 languages are still in use and 12 are taught to children; the Australian national surveys (NILS2 and NILS3) report 120 or more traditional languages "still spoken" using a looser definition, with only a handful strong. Choose one, and name it. [AIATSIS-NILS] [INDIGENOUS-GOV] The 2020 national report PDF could not be opened.
- Amano et al. (2014, Proceedings of the Royal Society B) found about 25 percent of languages threatened by species-style criteria, and linked recent speaker declines to high economic growth. Different method, so not used as a headline. [AMANO-2014]
- This should be written honestly but briefly: colonial languages spread alongside the loss of Indigenous ones, but the global picture also involves schools, roads, markets and states of every kind.

Card description (draft): "About 4 in 10 of the world's languages are endangered, and many have fewer than 1,000 speakers. Colonial rule and forced schooling pushed many Indigenous languages out of use, and the pressure continues today."
