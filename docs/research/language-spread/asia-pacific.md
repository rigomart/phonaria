# How languages spread: South Asia, East and Southeast Asia, Oceania

Research notes for the "How languages spread" page. Scope: South Asia, East and Southeast Asia, Australia, New Guinea and the Pacific. Researched 2026-10-17. Years are CE (positive) or BCE (negative). "BP" means years before 1950; "years ago" is used loosely in card text.

Conventions used below:

- `year` is one representative number for the era indicator, not a claim of precision.
- Coordinates are decimal degrees (lat, lon). Radii are rough.
- Spread destinations are schematic arrow targets, not routes.
- Citation keys in square brackets point to the Bibliography. Where a claim rests on one source, or a source that is a talk, preprint or news piece, it is marked WEAK.
- Nature, Science, PNAS and Britannica pages were not directly readable from the research environment (HTTP 403). For those papers the abstracts were read via PubMed / Europe PMC, which carry the publisher's abstract verbatim. Full texts actually read: Kolipakam 2018, Tao 2023, Robbeets 2021, Adelaar 2016, Pittayaporn 2014, Sidwell 2015 slides, van Driem 2018 and 2006. Where a claim needs more than an abstract, this is noted.
- Glottolog counts were computed from the Glottolog 5.3 CLDF data release, because glottolog.org itself was unreachable. These counts include extinct and poorly attested languages, so they run higher than counts of living languages.

## Sino-Tibetan

- **id:** `sino-tibetan`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** central estimates range from about 5,900 years ago (Zhang) to about 7,200 years ago (Sagart); the Zhang interval alone is 4,200 to 7,800 years ago
- **year:** -4500
- **location:** (35.5, 112.5), radius about 400 km. Middle Yellow River basin, around the Henan, Shanxi and Shaanxi border area.
- **location uncertainty:** The "northern" origin comes from two dated language-tree studies. A rival view puts the origin far to the southwest, in Sichuan or the eastern Himalaya, so the point could be about 1,500 km away. Linguistic trees give a region, not a village.
- **spread to:**
  - (31.0, 91.0) Tibetan plateau
  - (25.0, 97.0) hills of northern Myanmar / Yunnan, where Tibeto-Burman languages are most varied
  - (30.5, 114.0) middle Yangtze, for Chinese (Sinitic) spreading south
- **claims:**
  1. A language-tree study of Sino-Tibetan using sound correspondences and cognates places the origin with north Chinese millet farmers about 7,200 years ago, and links it to the late Cishan and early Yangshao cultures. [Sagart 2019]
  2. A separate study of 109 languages dates the first split to about 4,200 to 7,800 years ago (average about 5,900), supports a northern origin in the Yellow River basin, and finds Chinese (Sinitic) as one side of the first split and the Tibeto-Burman languages as the other. It reads the result as fitting the idea that farming helped spread the languages. [Zhang 2019]
  3. Sino-Tibetan is one of the world's largest families, spoken by nearly 1.4 billion people. [Sagart 2019]
  4. Another view puts the family's home in the eastern Himalaya and the Indo-Burmese borderlands, because that is where its branches are most varied. [van Driem 2018; van Driem 2006]
- **split note:** Scholars are divided on where the family began. Two dated studies (Sagart and Zhang) point to the Yellow River basin and millet farming. Zhang's own abstract describes a competing "southwestern" view, with an earlier start (before 9,000 years ago) in southwest Sichuan or northeast India. George van Driem argues for an eastern Himalayan homeland from the variety of branches there, and does not use the label "Sino-Tibetan" (he prefers "Trans-Himalayan"). The two northern studies also differ by about 1,300 years in their central dates, although their ranges overlap.
- **card description:** "Chinese, Tibetan and Burmese share an ancestor. Language trees suggest it began among millet farmers along the Yellow River in northern China, though some scholars place the start in the Himalayan foothills instead."
- **WEAK / unverified:** The destination arrows are schematic. No source read here gives dates for the Tibetan or Myanmar legs, so the page should not date them.

## Old Chinese writing (Shang oracle bones)

- **id:** `chinese-oracle-bones`
- **kind:** language
- **date:** about 1250 BCE onward
- **year:** -1250
- **location:** (36.1, 114.3), radius about 30 km. Anyang, Henan, the late Shang capital.
- **location uncertainty:** Low. The site is well known. The language is the Shang-era ancestor of Chinese, spoken across a wider area than the capital.
- **spread to:** none needed.
- **claims:**
  1. The Anyang period of the Shang dynasty runs from about 1250 BCE to about 1050 BCE, and it "begins just before the earliest evidence for writing in what is now China". [Anderson 2015]
  2. Chinese writing is not securely attested earlier than this. The Anyang texts are the earliest well-dated body of Chinese writing. [Anderson 2015] (WEAK for "not securely attested earlier": it rests on one dissertation abstract, which says only that the period begins just before the earliest evidence for writing.)
- **split note:** None needed for the 1250 BCE date. Whether earlier symbols on Neolithic pottery count as writing is argued over, and no source read here settles it.
- **card description:** "The oldest clear Chinese writing is scratched on animal bones and turtle shells, used by Shang kings around 1250 BCE to ask questions of their ancestors."
- **WEAK / dropped:** A Mandarin "today" stop was dropped because no readable source for the speaker count could be reached (Ethnologue and Britannica returned 403). The family-level figure of about 1.4 billion speakers (Sagart 2019) is verified and can be used instead.

## Austronesian (Taiwan origin)

- **id:** `austronesian-taiwan`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** about 5,200 years ago (language tree); speakers reached Taiwan about 6,000 years ago (genetic study)
- **year:** -3300
- **location:** (23.7, 121.0), radius about 200 km. Taiwan.
- **location uncertainty:** Taiwan as the source is well supported by linguistic work. The people who reached Taiwan came from the southeast Chinese coast, which is a looser origin. The deeper link to mainland ancestors is less certain.
- **spread to:**
  - (17.0, 121.0) northern Philippines
  - (1.0, 114.0) Borneo (and from there west to Madagascar; see Malagasy)
  - (-4.5, 152.0) Bismarck Archipelago (see Lapita)
- **claims:**
  1. A tree of 400 Austronesian languages places the origin in Taiwan about 5,230 years ago, and shows spreading in bursts with pauses in between, linked to new technology and social change. This favoured a fast "from Taiwan" spread over an older, slower "from Wallacea" alternative. [Gray 2009]
  2. Austronesian languages are spread "across half the globe, from Easter Island to Madagascar", and the expansion began 4,000 to 5,000 years ago with roots in Taiwan. [Lipson 2014]
  3. DNA from an 8,000-year-old skeleton and from 550 Taiwanese genomes suggests early Austronesians arrived in northern Taiwan about 6,000 years ago, spread to the south, and left Taiwan about 4,000 years ago for Island Southeast Asia, Madagascar and Oceania. [Ko 2014]
  4. The "most widely accepted model" links the Lapita settlers of Remote Oceania (about 3,000 years ago) to a wider spread from China via Taiwan about 4,500 to 6,000 years ago. [Almeida 2025]
- **split note:** The language and archaeology evidence for a Taiwan start is widely accepted, and Ko's abstract itself says the genetic evidence has been "more controversial". Gray's paper tested a "slow boat from Wallacea" model and found it fits the language data less well. Lipson found that western Island Southeast Asian groups carry ancestry from an Austroasiatic-related source, so the route may have run through mainland Southeast Asia. Dates differ by source (5,230 years ago for the language tree, about 6,000 years ago for arrival in Taiwan).
- **card description:** "Austronesian languages, from Tagalog to Hawaiian to Malagasy, trace back to Taiwan about 5,000 years ago. Seafarers carried them island by island across half the globe."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The brief's phrase "widest-spread family before 1500 CE" is a superlative that no source read here states. Lipson's "half the globe, from Easter Island to Madagascar" is the closest verified wording. Use that, not "widest". Glottolog 5.3 lists about 1,270 Austronesian languages (including extinct ones), which is the figure to use for size.

## Austronesian in the Philippines and Island Southeast Asia

- **id:** `austronesian-island-southeast-asia`
- **kind:** branch
- **date:** from about 4,000 years ago
- **year:** -2200
- **location:** (14.0, 121.5), radius about 600 km. Philippines.
- **location uncertainty:** The Philippines as the first stop after Taiwan is the standard picture. The exact first landfall and the order of later movements across Borneo, Sulawesi and Java are less certain.
- **spread to:**
  - (1.0, 114.0) Borneo
  - (-2.0, 120.0) Sulawesi
  - (-7.5, 110.0) Java
  - (13.4, 144.8) Mariana Islands (reached by about 3,500 years ago)
- **claims:**
  1. After arriving in Taiwan about 6,000 years ago, early Austronesians left Taiwan about 4,000 years ago to spread through Island Southeast Asia. [Ko 2014]
  2. All sampled Austronesian groups carry ancestry closer to Taiwanese Indigenous peoples than to any mainland group today. Western Island Southeast Asians also carry ancestry from a source related to Austroasiatic speakers. [Lipson 2014]
  3. People reached the Mariana Islands in the western Pacific by about 3,500 years ago, contemporaneous with or earlier than the first settlement of Polynesia. [Pugach 2021]
- **split note:** The Marianas result comes from DNA. The abstract says the origin of the first Marianas settlers is uncertain (Philippines, Indonesia, New Guinea or the Bismarck Archipelago have each been suggested). A direct sail from Taiwan or the Philippines is therefore not settled.
- **card description:** "From Taiwan the language spread south through the Philippines and into Borneo, Sulawesi and Java over the next couple of thousand years, carried by sailors and farmers."
- **WEAK:** The Borneo, Sulawesi and Java arrows are schematic. No source read here gives a date for each.

## Lapita and Remote Oceania

- **id:** `austronesian-lapita-remote-oceania`
- **kind:** branch
- **date:** about 3,000 years ago (settlement of Remote Oceania estimated at about 3,200 years ago, with a range of 2,700 to 3,750)
- **year:** -1000
- **location:** (-4.5, 152.0), radius about 500 km. Bismarck Archipelago and nearby northern New Guinea coast, the likely staging area.
- **location uncertainty:** The Lapita staging area is inferred from archaeology and mitochondrial DNA lineages that arose around the northern New Guinea coast. The abstracts read do not name a single island.
- **spread to:**
  - (-17.7, 168.3) Vanuatu
  - (-17.8, 178.0) Fiji
  - (-21.2, -175.2) Tonga
  - (-13.8, -172.1) Samoa
- **claims:**
  1. People of the Lapita culture appeared in the South Pacific around 3,000 years ago, "the beginning of the last major human dispersal to unpopulated lands". Ancient DNA from Vanuatu (about 3,100 to 2,700 years ago) and Tonga (about 2,700 to 2,300 years ago) shows the first settlers were nearly all East Asian in ancestry, with little or no Papuan ancestry. [Skoglund 2016]
  2. Settlement of Remote Oceania is dated to about 3,200 years ago by DNA-based dating that matches radiocarbon dates. [Almeida 2025]
  3. Papuan-related people reached Vanuatu from about 2,500 years ago and nearly replaced the Lapita ancestry there. The islanders still speak Austronesian languages. The authors call population replacement with language continuity "extremely rare, if not unprecedented". [Posth 2018]
- **split note:** The simple picture, "Austronesian speakers sailed from Taiwan, then out to Lapita", is the most widely accepted, per Almeida's abstract, but DNA studies keep finding that ancestry in the region is mixed. Almeida says the Lapita settlers may have carried lineages that arose in New Guinea, with migrants from Taiwan or Island Southeast Asia as a trigger. The page should say that people and language travelled together at first, and that later movements changed who the speakers were without changing the language family.
- **card description:** "About 3,000 years ago, voyagers known for their stamped pottery sailed east from the Bismarck Islands into islands no one had lived on before, taking an Austronesian language with them."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The brief's "Lapita culture about 3,000 years ago" is supported. A separate, older Lapita start in the Bismarcks (about 3,300 years ago) is widely quoted but no source read here states it, so it is left out.

## East Polynesia (Hawaii, Rapa Nui, Aotearoa)

- **id:** `polynesia-east`
- **kind:** branch
- **date:** Society Islands about 1025 to 1120 CE; then the remaining islands (including Hawaii, New Zealand and Rapa Nui) about 1190 to 1290 CE
- **year:** 1100
- **location:** (-17.5, -149.5), radius about 500 km. Society Islands (Tahiti area) as the first East Polynesian landfall.
- **location uncertainty:** The Society Islands as the first East Polynesian settlement is the result of a radiocarbon re-analysis; the source of the voyagers further west (Samoa, Tonga area) is inferred.
- **spread to:**
  - (20.0, -156.0) Hawaii
  - (-27.1, -109.4) Rapa Nui (Easter Island)
  - (-39.0, 176.0) New Zealand (Aotearoa)
- **claims:**
  1. A review of 1,434 radiocarbon dates shows East Polynesia was settled in two phases: the Society Islands about 1025 to 1120 CE, then, after 70 to 265 years, one big pulse to all remaining islands about 1190 to 1290 CE. This is about four centuries later than the older view. [Wilmshurst 2011]
  2. The pulse included New Zealand, Hawaii and Rapa Nui. The authors say the short timeline explains why East Polynesian culture, biology and language are so uniform. [Wilmshurst 2011]
  3. Radiocarbon dates from Anakena on Rapa Nui indicate the island was colonized late, about 1200 CE. [Hunt & Lipo 2006]
- **split note:** Older chronologies put East Polynesian settlement centuries or even a millennium earlier. Wilmshurst's abstract says earlier proposals "varied by more than 1000 years" and argues those relied on unreliable samples. The short chronology is now the main view in this evidence, but Hawaii has no separate date in the sources read here (it falls inside the 1190 to 1290 pulse). Treat any single year for Hawaii as approximate.
- **card description:** "Around 1000 CE, Polynesian sailors reached the Society Islands and, within a century or two, Hawaii, Rapa Nui and New Zealand: the last large places on Earth where people settled."
- **WEAK:** Hawaii has no island-specific date in the verified sources (only membership in the later pulse).

## Aotearoa and Māori

- **id:** `maori-aotearoa`
- **kind:** language
- **date:** about 1280 CE
- **year:** 1280
- **location:** (-38.3, 176.5), radius about 600 km. North Island, New Zealand (Glottolog's point for Māori).
- **location uncertainty:** The first landing site is not known. The point is a language centre, not a landing place.
- **spread to:** none needed.
- **claims:**
  1. Radiocarbon dating of rat-gnawed seeds and rat bones shows the Pacific rat, which people carried on their canoes, reached both main islands of New Zealand about 1280 CE, "a millennium later than previously assumed". [Wilmshurst 2008]
  2. This matches the earliest dated archaeological sites and the first signs of human-caused extinctions. [Wilmshurst 2008]
  3. The wider East Polynesian dates agree: the last pulse of settlement was about 1190 to 1290 CE. [Wilmshurst 2011]
- **split note:** None among the sources read. The date is derived from the rat's arrival, which is a stand-in for people, not a human bone. The abstract says it "matches" the earliest archaeological sites. The full text was not read.
- **card description:** "New Zealand was one of the last places settled. Polynesian voyagers arrived around 1280 CE, and their language became Māori."
- **WEAK:** The 1280 date rests on the rat proxy plus the 2011 re-analysis. The abstract of the 2008 paper was truncated in the tool output at the end ("This matches with the earliest-dated archaeological sites, human-induced faunal extinction..."), so the exact closing wording was not seen.

## Malagasy (Madagascar)

- **id:** `malagasy`
- **kind:** language
- **date:** migration from Borneo most probably 7th century CE; settlement of Madagascar most probably 8th century CE (one linguist's estimate); a DNA study says "the last millennium"
- **year:** 800
- **location:** (-2.1, 115.1), radius about 300 km. Southeast Barito area of southern Borneo, home of Ma'anyan (Glottolog's point for Ma'anyan). Madagascar is the destination.
- **location uncertainty:** The language points to southern Borneo. The DNA does not. The Ma'anyan people themselves do not show the clearest genetic link to Malagasy, so the "origin" point is a language link, not necessarily a place the founders lived.
- **spread to:**
  - (-5.0, 39.0) coastal East Africa (stop-over)
  - (-11.7, 43.3) Comoros
  - (-19.0, 47.0) Madagascar
- **claims:**
  1. Malagasy is most closely related to the Southeast Barito languages of southern Borneo, of which Ma'anyan is one. Dahl first proposed the Borneo link in 1951, and genetic work in 2005 helped it gain acceptance. [Adelaar 2016]
  2. Adelaar distinguishes three events: contacts between East Africa and Island Southeast Asia from at least 2,300 years ago; migration from Borneo to East Africa most probably in the 7th century CE and settlement of Madagascar most probably in the 8th century CE; and trade contact after that. [Adelaar 2016]
  3. All Malagasy people have both Bantu (African) and Austronesian descent. Mixing happened recently, suggesting rapid settlement during the last thousand years. [Pierron 2017]
  4. Language connects Madagascar to the Ma'anyan of southern Borneo, but genome data show Malagasy do not have a clear genetic link to the Ma'anyan, only to a range of southern Bornean and other Island Southeast Asian groups. [Kusuma 2016]
  5. Crops from sites in Madagascar and the Comoros are dominated by Asian species, while East African coast sites are dominated by African crops. This supports Austronesian settlement reaching Madagascar by an indirect route via the Comoros and/or East Africa. [Crowther 2016]
- **split note:** The language and DNA tell slightly different stories about the homeland (Ma'anyan in the language; a mixture of southern Borneo groups in the DNA). Scholars also disagree on whether settlers came directly across the Indian Ocean or by an indirect route through East Africa. Adelaar and Crowther favor indirect. Dates have ranged from the 5th century CE (Dahl) to the 7th and 8th (Adelaar), and one DNA study says only "the last millennium". Adelaar himself notes crops in Africa show much older contact, and that Madagascar had some human presence about 2,300 years ago, of unknown ancestry.
- **card description:** "Malagasy, spoken in Madagascar off Africa's east coast, is most closely related to languages in Borneo, thousands of miles away. Sailors probably crossed the Indian Ocean about 1,200 years ago."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The brief says "first millennium CE"; the sources support roughly 700 to 1000 CE, and a source for the earlier edge (Dahl, 5th century) was not read first-hand (Adelaar's summary of it was). The Banjar-as-source claim from a later genome study was seen only in a search summary and was not verified, so it is left out.

## Dravidian

- **id:** `dravidian`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** about 4,500 years ago (interval about 3,000 to 6,500 years ago)
- **year:** -2500
- **location:** (20.0, 79.0), radius about 800 km. Central peninsular India, a placeholder. The origin is not known.
- **location uncertainty:** High. The authors state that "neither the geographical origin of the Dravidian language homeland nor its exact dispersal through time are known". Proposals include Sindh, Gujarat and eastern Maharashtra (as places Dravidian was once spoken), the lower Godavari basin (as a homeland), and a link to the Indus civilization (a supposition).
- **spread to:**
  - (10.5, 78.8) Tamil Nadu (Tamil)
  - (29.0, 66.5) Balochistan (Brahui)
  - (24.5, 86.5) Chota Nagpur plateau (Kurukh)
- **claims:**
  1. A study of about 80 Dravidian varieties spoken by 220 million people finds the family is about 4,500 years old, with the main branches North, Central, South I and South II. [Kolipakam 2018]
  2. The authors note the family influenced Indo-Aryan and Munda, "despite their current restricted range". [Kolipakam 2018]
  3. Uncertainty about how the four branches are related to each other is "considerable". [Kolipakam 2018]
  4. A link to the Indus Valley civilization has been suggested (Krishnamurti links Proto-Dravidian to it; Parpola links the Indus script to Dravidian), but the study's authors present it as a supposition. [Kolipakam 2018]
- **split note:** The 4,500 date has a wide interval, about 3,000 to 6,500 years ago in the study's analyses. The Indus connection is argued, not proven: the Indus script is undeciphered, and the language of the Indus cities is unknown. The study treats it as a supposition. Southworth's proposals (a Southern Neolithic link, a Godavari homeland) are alternatives.
- **card description:** "Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam belong to a family at least 4,500 years old. Where it began is not known, and a link to the Indus civilization is a guess, not a finding."
- **WEAK:** The Indus-link claim rests on one paper's summary of earlier scholars (Krishnamurti, Parpola, Southworth), which I read in full text. A search turned up several popular and advocacy sources that call the link either proven or false; none were used.

## Tamil

- **id:** `tamil`
- **kind:** language
- **date:** first recorded by about 254 BCE (an old-edition encyclopedia places the earliest literary monuments in the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE)
- **year:** -250
- **location:** (10.5, 78.8), radius about 300 km. Tamil Nadu, with a southward extension to northern Sri Lanka (Glottolog's point for Tamil).
- **location uncertainty:** Low for the modern language. The language's earlier range is not known.
- **spread to:** (8.0, 80.7) northern Sri Lanka.
- **claims:**
  1. Tamil was "recorded first in 254 BCE", which the Dravidian tree study used as a minimum age for the South I branch (at least 2,250 years). [Kolipakam 2018]
  2. An older Encyclopaedia Britannica article on Dravidian languages, copied on a University of Pennsylvania course page, says the four major Dravidian literary languages have scripts and literatures "dating from the pre-Christian Era", and that Tamil's earliest literary monuments belong "roughly to the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC". [Britannica (mirror)]
- **split note:** Some excavations in Tamil Nadu (Keezhadi) have been reported as dating Tamil writing to the 6th century BCE, but the link between the inscribed pottery and the dated layers is contested. The sources were news and blog summaries, so this is not used.
- **card description:** "Tamil has been written down for well over two thousand years, one of the longest records of any living language in India."
- **WEAK:** Only two sources, one a secondary mirror of an old encyclopedia edition. "Continuously written" was dropped (no source). The card text says "well over two thousand years" which both sources support. The Keezhadi 6th-century claim was seen only in non-scholarly summaries and is not used.

## Brahui (optional card, can fold into Dravidian)

- **id:** `brahui`
- **kind:** language
- **date:** unknown. The language is present in Balochistan today. No reliable arrival date exists.
- **year:** null (no defensible year; suggest anchoring to the Dravidian card)
- **location:** (29.0, 66.5), radius about 250 km. Central Balochistan, Pakistan (Glottolog's point).
- **location uncertainty:** Low for today's speakers. High for how and when the language got there.
- **spread to:** none.
- **claims:**
  1. Brahui is a Dravidian language spoken in Pakistan and Afghanistan; in the best-supported tree it splits off first within the North Dravidian group. [Kolipakam 2018]
  2. Brahui speakers are genetically closer to neighbouring Indo-European-speaking Pakistani groups than to Dravidian speakers of southern India. The authors favour a model in which a Dravidian-speaking population was largely absorbed by Indo-European migrants about 3,000 years ago, while the Dravidian language persisted. [Pagani 2017]
- **split note:** Whether Brahui is a leftover of a once wider Dravidian range, or arrived more recently from central India, is disputed. Pagani favours the leftover model, on genetic grounds. Their evidence does not show the language arrived with a migration from the south.
- **card description:** "Brahui, spoken in Balochistan in Pakistan, is a Dravidian language more than 1,500 km from the rest of the family. It may be a leftover from a time when Dravidian languages were spoken more widely in the northwest."
- **WEAK:** The "more than 1,500 km" figure came from a search summary of a Wikipedia-sourced statement. It matches geography (Balochistan to Karnataka) but is not cited. Remove it from the card if a source is wanted. Speaker numbers differ across sources, from about half a million in one chapter to several million in others, so none is given.

## Austroasiatic

- **id:** `austroasiatic`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** a split between the eastern and western branches about 7,000 years ago and all main branches present by about 4,000 years ago (conference slides); oldest ancient genomes of Austroasiatic-type ancestry about 4,100 years ago
- **year:** -3000
- **location:** (17.0, 103.0), radius about 800 km. Middle Mekong region (Laos, northeast Thailand), one candidate.
- **location uncertainty:** High. Homeland proposals span northern India, western India, the Bay of Bengal, southern China, the middle Yangtze, Shandong, and mainland Southeast Asia.
- **spread to:**
  - (16.0, 108.0) central Vietnam coast (Vietnamese)
  - (12.5, 105.0) Cambodia (Khmer)
  - (22.5, 86.0) eastern India (Munda, e.g. Santali)
  - (5.0, 101.5) Malay Peninsula (Aslian languages)
- **claims:**
  1. The family is mainly found in mainland Southeast Asia, with about 130 languages and about 13 branches, and it was the area's main language base before other families arrived. Sidwell's 2015 analysis dates a basic east-west split to about 7,000 years ago, with all branches formed by about 4,000 years ago. [Sidwell 2015] (WEAK: conference slides, a work in progress.)
  2. Ancient DNA from early farmers at Man Bac in Vietnam (4,100 to 1,700 years ago) shows a mix of southern Chinese farmer ancestry and older hunter-gatherer ancestry. This is characteristic of Austroasiatic speakers today, and similar ancestry as far south as Indonesia suggests a wide early spread. [Lipson 2018]
  3. Indian Austroasiatic (Munda) speakers appear to descend from a dispersal out of Southeast Asia, followed by heavy mixing with local Indian groups. This is based on a Y-chromosome lineage that is more varied in Southeast Asia. [Chaubey 2011]
  4. Sidwell argues for a radiation out of the Mekong valley and treats Munda as a highly innovative outlier. [Sidwell 2010]
- **split note:** Scholars are closely split on where it began. Sidwell lists proposals for northern, western and eastern India, the Bay of Bengal (van Driem, Diffloth), southern China, the middle Yangtze, Shandong, and Southeast Asia (himself). His favoured Mekong homeland is contested by supporters of a Yangtze origin. Van Driem argues the point of dispersal lay between South Asia and mainland Southeast Asia. The link to rice is argued by linguists but contested, and Sidwell does not rest his case on it.
- **card description:** "Vietnamese, Khmer and the Munda languages of India belong to one old family. Its homeland is hotly debated: some place it by the Mekong, some in southern China, some near the Bay of Bengal."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The 7,000 / 4,000 BP dates come from a conference slide set (Sidwell 2015), not a published paper. The family-and-rice-farming link from the brief is not confirmed by a source I could read (Sidwell 2010 does not make rice central), so it is not stated as fact. The Yangtze / Pearl River details came from an encyclopedia summary and were not used.

## Tai-Kadai (Kra-Dai)

- **id:** `tai-kadai`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** first split about 4,000 years ago (interval about 2,700 to 5,500)
- **year:** -2000
- **location:** (22.5, 109.0), radius about 300 km. Coastal Guangxi and Guangdong, southern China.
- **location uncertainty:** The coastal Guangxi-Guangdong area was the most probable homeland in the tree analysis, with 47% posterior support, so it is a best guess, not a certainty.
- **spread to:**
  - (19.2, 109.7) Hainan
  - (25.0, 104.0) inland Yunnan and Guizhou, then southwest
  - (16.0, 102.0) mainland Southeast Asia
- **claims:**
  1. A Bayesian tree of 100 Kra-Dai languages dates the first split to about 4,000 years ago and places the homeland in the Guangxi-Guangdong coastal area, with early dispersal across the Qiongzhou Strait to Hainan, inland to Yunnan and Guizhou, and south into mainland Southeast Asia. [Tao 2023]
  2. The authors link the spread to farming and to climate change in the late Holocene. [Tao 2023]
  3. The Kra-Dai languages sit among the settlements of four other families: Austronesian, Austroasiatic, Sino-Tibetan and Hmong-Mien. [Tao 2023]
- **split note:** Tao's date (about 4,000 years ago) is younger than the 5,000 to 6,000 years some earlier scholars proposed, per their summary. Whether Kra-Dai is related to Austronesian is an open question, which the authors name among the "unresolved issues". The homeland on the coast competes with inland proposals.
- **card description:** "Thai and Lao belong to a family that probably began on the south China coast about 4,000 years ago, and later spread west and south into mainland Southeast Asia."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The Taiwan and middle-Yangtze proposals mentioned in a search summary were not verified and are left out.

## Thai and Lao spread (Southwestern Tai)

- **id:** `southwestern-tai`
- **kind:** branch
- **date:** began spreading south sometime between the 8th and 10th centuries CE (one study); other scholars range from the 1st century BCE to the 13th century CE
- **year:** 900
- **location:** (22.0, 104.5), radius about 300 km. Southern China / northern Vietnam border area, the departure zone.
- **location uncertainty:** Medium. The study describes a southward movement from southern China; the exact starting place is not given in the abstract.
- **spread to:**
  - (18.0, 102.5) Laos
  - (15.0, 100.5) central Thailand (Chao Phraya basin)
  - (22.0, 97.5) Shan hills, Myanmar
  - (26.5, 94.5) northeast India (Assam)
- **claims:**
  1. Chinese loanwords in Proto-Southwestern Tai fall into four layers, up to the Tang era. The study proposes that Southwestern Tai languages began to spread southward sometime during the 8th and 10th centuries CE. [Pittayaporn 2014]
  2. The Tai-speaking area today stretches from northern Vietnam to Northeast India. [Pittayaporn 2014]
  3. Other proposed dates for the start of the migration include the 1st century BCE, the 10th century (Diller), the 12th century (Saraya), and before the 13th century (Diskul). [Pittayaporn 2014]
- **split note:** Closely split. The 8th to 10th century estimate comes from loanword layers and is the best argued in the sources read, but proposals run from the 1st century BCE to the 13th century CE, and the same paper says the date is unclear. A conference version gave the 9th to 11th century.
- **card description:** "Thai and Lao speakers moved south out of southern China into the river valleys of mainland Southeast Asia, probably starting about 1,200 years ago."
- **WEAK:** Single study for the narrowed date (though it also lists rival dates). The Assam arrow rests only on the study's description of the present Tai area.

## Japonic (and Koreanic)

- **id:** `japonic-koreanic`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** farming and a language moved from the Korean peninsula to Japan after about 3,000 years ago (BP)
- **year:** -1000
- **location:** (35.0, 127.0), radius about 250 km. Southern Korean peninsula.
- **location uncertainty:** Medium. Japonic and Koreanic are treated as separate families, and no source read here pins down where the ancestors of Japonic lived before the move.
- **spread to:**
  - (33.5, 130.5) northern Kyushu
  - (35.0, 135.0) Kinki / central Honshu
- **claims:**
  1. Rice, wheat and other crops reached the Korean peninsula by the early Bronze Age (3300 to 2800 BP) and Japan "after 3000 BP". Genomes from Yayoi farmers can be modelled as local Jomon ancestry mixed with newcomer ancestry from the Korean side, and the authors read this as "massive migration from Korea into Japan". [Robbeets 2021]
  2. Ancient genomes from Japan show rice farming was introduced by people with Northeast Asian ancestry, after the Jomon hunter-gatherers who had been isolated for millennia. [Cooke 2021]
  3. Japonic, Koreanic, Tungusic, Mongolic and Turkic are five families that are not in dispute. Whether they share a single ancestor ("Transeurasian", formerly "Altaic") is the contested question. [Robbeets 2021]
- **split note:** Closely split on one question: whether Japonic and Koreanic share a deeper ancestry with Tungusic, Mongolic and Turkic. Robbeets and co-authors argue they do and tie it to early millet farming. A 2022 critique (a preprint, not yet peer reviewed as far as I could find) by Tian, Jacques, Ryder and colleagues argues the language data do not meet standard historical-linguistics tests and that neither the genetic nor the archaeological data support the claim. Robbeets's group replied in another preprint. A 1999 paper defends Altaic. Vovin reviewed the main Altaic dictionary critically after first supporting the idea. Glottolog 5.3 lists Japonic and Koreanic as separate top-level families and has no Transeurasian family. The page should present Japonic arriving with Yayoi rice farmers and leave "Transeurasian" out of the card text.
- **card description:** "Japanese probably came to the Japanese islands about 3,000 years ago with rice farmers from the Korean peninsula, who mixed with the hunter-gatherers already there."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The brief says the mainstream rejects Altaic/Transeurasian. No source I could read states that as a survey result. What is verified: the dispute is open (Robbeets vs. Tian et al.), Vovin is a prominent critic, and Glottolog does not use the grouping. Do not say "most linguists reject it" without a source. The Yayoi arrival date rests on Robbeets (crops arriving "after 3000 BP") and Cooke (transformation "in the past 3000 years"); both are about farming and genes, not about the language itself. That a language came with the farmers is an inference.

## Pama-Nyungan (Australia)

- **id:** `pama-nyungan`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** origin about 5,700 years ago (authors' summary); project page gives 5,000 to 7,000 years ago
- **year:** -3700
- **location:** (-17.7, 139.5), radius about 300 km. Gulf Plains, south of the Gulf of Carpentaria (near Burketown, Queensland).
- **location uncertainty:** The tree method finds "strong and robust support" for the Gulf Plains region, but this is a region on a map built from word lists of 306 languages, not a precise site.
- **spread to:** (schematic, direction not verified)
  - (-25.0, 128.0) Western Desert
  - (-22.0, 118.0) Pilbara, northwest
  - (-33.0, 147.0) southeast Australia
- **claims:**
  1. Word lists from 306 Pama-Nyungan languages and a map-based tree method show "strong and robust support" for an origin in the Gulf Plains region during the mid-Holocene, implying quick replacement of non-Pama-Nyungan languages. [Bouckaert 2018]
  2. The authors say the languages were probably carried as part of an expanding set of cultural innovations, which let incoming groups absorb existing hunter-gatherers, since genetic evidence does not show a large population spread. [Bouckaert 2018]
  3. Pama-Nyungan is the world's largest hunter-gatherer language family. By the authors' own summary it covers about 90% of the mainland and more than 75% of Australia's roughly 400 languages. [Bouckaert 2018; Bouckaert et al. 2018 blog] (WEAK for the 90% and 75% figures: the authors' own plain-language post, not the paper.)
  4. People had been in Australia far longer. An excavation at Madjedbebe in northern Australia dates occupation to about 65,000 years ago. A DNA study finds Indigenous Australian groups diverged from New Guinean groups about 47,000 years ago. A 2025 mitochondrial study backs a "long chronology", first arrivals by about 60,000 to 65,000 years ago. [Clarkson 2017; Silcocks 2023; Gandini 2025]
  5. The Australian Government's census-based report counts about 250 Indigenous languages at the time Australia was colonised. [ABS 2010]
- **split note:** Closely split on cause, not on the main result. Before the 2018 paper, some argued for rapid spread from the Gulf Plains in the mid-Holocene, others for spread from refuges after the last ice age, and a controversial few for spread at the very first human arrival. The authors favour the first and think 50,000 years is too long for related languages to stay recognisable. Counts also differ: Australia's languages are given as about 250 at colonisation (government report) or about 400 (the 2018 authors' post). Glottolog 5.3 lists 388 languages in the Australian area, 249 of them Pama-Nyungan, which is about 64%.
- **card description:** "People lived in Australia for tens of thousands of years, but Pama-Nyungan, the family covering most of the continent, spread out from the Gulf of Carpentaria only about 5,700 years ago."
- **WEAK / dropped:** "About 90%" is from the authors' blog; a news article repeating it was not verified. The "250 languages" figure is from a 2005 survey as reported in the 2009-10 Year Book; an AIATSIS page said "over 250" in a search summary but could not be opened. Direction arrows are schematic: the project page colours regions by time of first arrival, but the text names no regions and times.

## Trans-New Guinea (and the label "Papuan")

- **id:** `trans-new-guinea`
- **kind:** family
- **date:** an expansion beginning about 6,000 to 10,000 years ago
- **year:** -6000
- **location:** (-6.0, 145.0), radius about 300 km. Central highlands of Papua New Guinea (proposed homeland).
- **location uncertainty:** High. The highlands homeland is "putative" and "tentatively thought" in the sources read.
- **spread to:** (schematic)
  - (-4.0, 137.5) west along the central ranges
  - (-8.0, 147.5) southeast Papua
- **claims:**
  1. New Guinea has the world's highest linguistic diversity, with more than 900 languages in at least 23 distinct language families. Trans-New Guinea is the world's third-largest family. [Greenhill 2015]
  2. A genetic study ties Trans-New Guinea to an expansion starting about 6,000 to 10,000 years ago from the central highlands of Papua New Guinea, presumably with the spread of agriculture. [Mona 2007]
  3. The TransNewGuinea.org project describes the family as possibly the third largest in the world, with about 400 languages across most of the interior, "tentatively thought to have originated with root-crop agriculture around 10,000 years ago", and says "vanishingly little is known about this family's history". [TransNewGuinea.org]
  4. "Papuan" is a geographic label for non-Austronesian languages of the New Guinea area, not a language family. Grouping them into one family has not been shown. [Pereltsvaig 2012; Greenhill 2015]
  5. Glottolog 5.3 lists 2,218 languages in the Papunesia area, of which 984 are not Austronesian, spread over 81 top-level groupings (this includes pidgins and creoles, so it overstates the non-Austronesian count slightly). [Glottolog 5.3 (my count)]
- **split note:** Closely split on the family's edges, not its existence. Pawley and Hammarström's 2018 chapter (read through search summaries only) gives a family of between 300 and 500 languages and leaves unclear languages unclassified; Ross (2005) argued for a reduced version; Evans (summarised in a search result, not read) argues some southern New Guinea groups are joined on little evidence. The age is also loose: 6,000 to 10,000 years ago.
- **card description:** "New Guinea has more languages than almost anywhere. Many belong to Trans-New Guinea, a large family that may have spread with farming from the highlands, though the family's edges are still debated."
- **WEAK / dropped:** The 300-500 languages and the Ross and Evans positions came from search summaries because the Pawley & Hammarström chapter and Pawley 2005 could not be downloaded (bot blocks). They are marked only in this note and not used as claims. "At least 23 families" is Greenhill's wording for New Guinea; Glottolog's count of 81 groupings uses a wider area, so the two should not be placed side by side. The "contested boundaries" point rests on search summaries of Pawley and Hammarström; it should be reconfirmed from the chapter if the card quotes it.
