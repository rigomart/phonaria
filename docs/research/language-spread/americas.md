## First peoples cross into the Americas

- id: `peopling-of-the-americas`
- kind: migration (this is a people stop, not a language family)
- date range: "Widely accepted by about 16,000 to 15,000 years ago; some claims reach about 23,000 years ago"
- era year: -13000 (about 15,000 years ago)
- location: (64.0, -165.0), the Bering Strait and Beringia region, radius ~1200 km.
- location uncertainty: High. Beringia is now mostly under the sea. The point marks the gateway, not a homeland. The ancestors came from northeast Asia, and the genetic evidence puts the key split from East Asians tens of thousands of years ago, well before arrival.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (45.8, -116.3) Cooper's Ferry, Idaho (Pacific Northwest side)
  - (32.8, -106.2) White Sands, New Mexico
  - (-41.5, -73.2) Monte Verde, southern Chile
- scholars split? Yes, on three things. When: whether people were here during the Ice Age peak (before about 20,000 years ago) or arrived only after it. How: along the Pacific coast, or through an inland corridor between the ice sheets. And whether one language family ("Amerind") covers most of the continent, which most language specialists reject.

Claims:

1. The first Americans came from northeast Asia by way of Beringia, the land that once joined Siberia and Alaska.
   - [Moreno-Mayar 2018]: opens by noting "broad agreement that the Americas were initially populated via Beringia"; the Alaskan infant genome (about 11,500 years old) shows the ancestors of Native Americans split from East Asians around 36,000 years ago, and that the main Native American branches diverged around 17,500 to 14,600 years ago, probably south of the ice sheets.
   - [Willerslev and Meltzer 2021]: ancient genomes show the first dispersal "involved the movement from northeast Asia of distinct and previously unknown populations", and nearly all ancient individuals are more closely related to living Indigenous Americans than to anyone elsewhere.
2. Monte Verde II in Chile, about 14,500 years old, is widely accepted. Cooper's Ferry in Idaho has stone points dated to about 16,000 years old by the excavating team; "widely accepted" is not shown for that date by the one study cited here. (Second check: the first pass called both "widely accepted".)
   - [Dillehay 2015]: its introduction treats Monte Verde II as "one valid human site" dated about 14,500 calendar years before present. The same paper reports possible older human activity at nearby Monte Verde I (about 18,500 to 14,500 years ago) that the authors call probable, not certain.
   - [Davis 2022]: a stone-tool assemblage at Cooper's Ferry dated to about 16,000 years ago, older than Clovis points, from a time when glaciers had closed off an inland land route. One study, by the team that excavated the site; no independent assessment was read.
3. Footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, have been dated to about 23,000 to 21,000 years ago, but that age is contested.
   - [Bennett 2021]: human footprints between seed layers, radiocarbon dated to about 23,000 to 21,000 years ago, during the Ice Age peak.
   - [Oviatt 2022]: argues the dated water-plant seeds may carry old carbon from the water, so the ages are less certain than reported; a seed sample collected in 1947 from a nearby spring returned about 7,400 years.
   - [Pigati 2023]: new dates on pollen and on sediment by a second method agree with the original range; the authors conclude the original timeline is "robust". Several of these authors also wrote the 2021 paper.
   - [Holliday 2025]: a further study with 26 new radiocarbon dates on mud, run by two outside labs, also supports an Ice Age age for the tracks.
4. The route south is debated. A Pacific coastal route is the leading alternative to an inland corridor, because the inland corridor was probably not usable by people before about 12,600 years ago.
   - [Pedersen 2016]: lake-sediment DNA and plant remains show steppe plants and big game only by about 12,600 years ago; the first Americans "are unlikely to have travelled by this route", though later groups may have.
   - [Davis 2022]: the Cooper's Ferry points resemble Late Upper Paleolithic tools from the northwest Pacific rim, which the authors take as a hint that early traditions came along the Pacific side.
5. The Americas contain dozens of language families and isolates, and Greenberg's "Amerind" grouping is rejected by most specialists.
   - [Glottolog]: about 84 families and 95 isolates in North and South America (extinct languages included).
   - [Gregersen 2025]: reviewing Campbell's 2024 book, mentions the Americas' diversity "with c. 170 recognized language families and isolates".
   - [Rogers 2021]: university lecture slides: most linguists do not accept Greenberg's three-group scheme and recognize roughly 150 to 180 independent families.
   - [EBSCO Native American families]: Amerind, "by far the largest group with 583 languages, was immediately denounced by traditionalists", who rejected the phylum and many of its branches; a majority of linguists reject or doubt Greenberg's method, though most assume large-scale relationships exist (paraphrase).

Draft card: "People first reached the Americas from northeast Asia, crossing the land around the Bering Strait. Most researchers agree they were south of the ice sheets by about 15,000 years ago. A few sites hint at much earlier arrivals, and that is still debated."

## Eskimo-Aleut and the Inuit spread

- id: `eskimo-aleut-inuit-thule`
- kind: family (the stop's arrows show the Inuit branch)
- date range: "Inuit ancestors spread east from Alaska in the 1200s, within a few centuries at most." A start near AD 1000 is the older view going back to Mathiassen (1927), which the newer radiocarbon work below has superseded; only the 1200s are supported by the sources here. (Second check: changed from "about AD 1000 to 1300", which put the superseded start in the range; the retired `inuit` stop's ~1000–1300 CE had the same problem.)
- era year: 1250
- location: (71.3, -156.8) northern Alaska coast near Point Barrow, radius ~600 km.
- location uncertainty: Moderate. Thule culture grew out of coastal Alaska, but the exact starting district is debated, and the wider Eskimo-Aleut family also reaches the Bering Strait, Siberia and the Aleutian Islands. The older family history before the Thule move is not covered here.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (69.5, -124.0) Amundsen Gulf / western Canadian Arctic
  - (66.0, -72.0) Baffin Island / eastern Canadian Arctic
  - (77.5, -70.0) northwest Greenland (Smith Sound)
  - (64.5, -51.5) west Greenland
- scholars split? On the start date only. A start near AD 1000 was long assumed. Newer radiocarbon work favors the 1200s. The direction (Alaska to Greenland) is not disputed.

Claims:

1. The Inuit languages form one long dialect chain from Alaska to Greenland. Neighbors understand each other, but speakers at the far ends do not.
   - [ANLC Comparative]: the Inuit language is "a continuum, or dialect chain" from Unalakleet on Norton Sound across northern Alaska and Canada to east Greenland; adjacent dialects are usually mutually intelligible, very distant ones are not.
   - [Gulløv and McGhee 2006]: note the "general uniformity" of Inuktitut/Inupiaq dialects from northwest Alaska to Greenland, which suggests the central and eastern Arctic Inuit trace their roots to eastward movements from North Alaska.
2. The Eskimo-Aleut family has Eskimo languages (Inuit and Yupik) and Aleut as its two main parts.
   - [ANLC Comparative]: describes Inuit and Yupik as the two Eskimo branches, with Sirenikski possibly a third.
   - [Glottolog]: lists Eskimo-Aleut as one family of 13 languages (extinct included) spanning Siberia and North America.
3. The Thule move east across the Arctic happened within a few centuries, and the best-dated evidence points to the 1200s.
   - [Friesen and Arnold 2008]: new radiocarbon dates from two early Thule sites on the western Arctic coast show neither was occupied before the thirteenth century AD, which the authors take as support for a thirteenth-century migration that was "more rapid and widespread" than previously believed.
   - [Gulløv and McGhee 2006]: re-reading the Eastern Arctic dates, the Ruin Island phase of northwest Greenland and Ellesmere Island is "probably" thirteenth century; they argue many older dates were misread because of driftwood and marine carbon.
   - [UAF Museum Thule]: the Thule tradition grew out of coastal Alaskan cultures and moved east into the Canadian High Arctic and parts of Greenland; the page credits Mathiassen (1927) with the original idea of a migration around AD 1000.
4. The newcomers largely replaced the earlier Arctic population genetically.
   - [Raghavan 2014]: ancient and modern genomes show Paleo-Eskimos (about 3000 BCE to 1300 CE) were a migration separate from other Native Americans and from the Inuit; the Paleo-Eskimo metapopulation survived in near-isolation for more than 4,000 years, "only to vanish around 700 years ago".
   - [Flegontov 2019]: Palaeo-Eskimos "were subsequently joined and largely displaced around 1,000 years ago by ancestors of the present-day Inuit and Yup'ik".
   - The two papers give different dates for the replacement: about 700 years ago (Raghavan) and about 1,000 years ago (Flegontov). Name the source with the date. (Second check.)

Draft card: "Inuit languages form one long chain of related dialects from Alaska to Greenland. The ancestors of today's Inuit spread east from Alaska across the Arctic quickly, probably in the 1200s." (Second check: "roughly between AD 1000 and 1300" replaced.)

## Na-Dene and the Athabaskan family

- id: `na-dene-athabaskan`
- kind: family
- date range: "No secure dates; spread over several thousand years"
- era year: -1000 (editorial placeholder for ordering only; no source gives a date, WEAK)
- location: (62.0, -140.0) Alaska/Yukon, radius ~900 km.
- location uncertainty: High. The family is centered on northwest North America today, but no source here pins down a homeland or a date. Glottolog's own center point (57.8, -134.7) sits in southeast Alaska/British Columbia and is only an average of language locations.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (42.0, -123.5) Pacific Northwest and northern California coast
  - (35.5, -109.5) Southwest (see next stop)
- scholars split? Yes, on the proposed Siberian link (Dene-Yeniseian). The link between Athabaskan, Eyak and Tlingit is well established.

Claims:

1. Athabaskan languages are spoken across Alaska and western Canada, with outliers on the Pacific coast and in the Southwest. Eyak and Tlingit are their closest relatives, forming the Athabaskan-Eyak-Tlingit (Na-Dene) family.
   - [ANLC Languages]: "Athabascan languages range through western Canada and down into the US Pacific Northwest and desert Southwest"; "the relationship of Eyak to both Athabascan and Tlingit is now well-established" (both re-read 2026-10-29).
   - [Glottolog]: lists 46 languages in Athabaskan-Eyak-Tlingit (extinct included).
2. Some linguists propose a link between Na-Dene and Yeniseian, a family now represented by Ket in central Siberia ("Dene-Yeniseian"). The idea is not settled.
   - [Vajda 2016]: summarizes the hypothesis as a proposed genealogical link; since 2006 new evidence has been published, but "Dene-Yeniseian cannot be accepted as a proven language family" until the evidence is expanded and tested.
   - [UAF news 2010]: Vajda reports more than 100 shared cognates and some shared verb endings; Kari and Potter edited the 2010 volume where the proposal appeared.
   - [Campbell 2011]: review of that volume in IJAL 77:445-451. The investigator confirmed the citation but did not read the text; the conclusion that the evidence does not yet support embracing the link comes from a search summary. WEAK for the exact wording; drop if needed.
3. If the link is real, it may not mean people moved from Asia into America. One analysis favors a spread out of Beringia with a later move back into Asia.
   - [Sicoli and Holton 2014]: phylogenetic tests of linguistic features favor "radiation out of Beringia with back-migration into central Asia" over a one-way move from Asia to North America. The authors say this is a statistical test of two hypotheses, not proof.
4. Genetically, Na-Dene and Eskimo-Aleut speakers share an ancestry strand from an earlier Siberian Arctic population.
   - [Flegontov 2019]: "Palaeo-Eskimo-related ancestry is ubiquitous among people who speak Na-Dene and Eskimo-Aleut languages."

Draft card: "The Dene (Athabaskan) languages are centered in the far northwest of North America, with branches on the Pacific coast and in the Southwest. Some linguists link them to a language family in Siberia. Others say the evidence is not strong enough yet."

## Navajo and Apache reach the Southwest

- id: `southern-athabaskan-southwest`
- kind: branch
- date range: "Debated: proposals run from after about AD 800 (Gordon) and the 1300s (Seymour's earliest dated sites) to after about AD 1450 (Crow Canyon); the first recognizably Navajo sites date from the mid-1500s." (Second check: changed from "Sometime between about AD 1200 and 1500". No source gives a 1200s arrival: Gordon's 1200-1400 is a gap in the record, not an arrival date, and Seymour's earliest dates are from the 1300s. The sources are competing accounts, not ranked here. The retired `navajo-apache` stop's ~1200–1500 CE had the same 1200s start.)
- era year: 1400
- location: (36.3, -109.4) Four Corners region (Navajo country), radius ~350 km.
- location uncertainty: High on date and route. Early sites are rare and hard to identify. The point marks where the languages are found, not where the move began.
- spread from / to: ancestors came from northwestern Canada or the Subarctic. Representative origin (58.0, -110.0), then destinations:
  - (36.3, -109.4) Navajo area
  - (33.9, -110.1) Western Apache area
- scholars split? Yes, on both timing and route. Proposals range from after about AD 800 to after about AD 1450. Routes through the Plains, the Rocky Mountain foothills, or the Intermountain West are all argued.

Claims:

1. Navajo and the Apache languages belong to the Athabaskan family, even though their nearest relatives are far to the north.
   - [Glottolog]: places Navajo (36.3, -109.4) and Western Apache (33.9, -110.1) in Athabaskan-Eyak-Tlingit.
   - [ANLC Languages]: the family extends into the desert Southwest.
2. The date of arrival is debated, and the sources disagree: Crow Canyon puts it after about 1450, Seymour dates the earliest ancestral Athabaskan sites to the 1300s and 1400s, and Gordon argues for a migration after 800. The first clearly Navajo sites in the Mesa Verde region are from the mid-1500s.
   - [Crow Canyon]: "Scholars disagree about the exact timing of these migrations, but it seems likely that Athabaskan speakers arrived in the Southwest after A.D. 1450 and were in the Mesa Verde region by at least the early 1500s"; the earliest recognizably Navajo sites there "date from the mid-1500s" (re-read 2026-10-29).
   - [Gordon]: a Carleton University researcher proposes a "post-800 AD southern Athapaskan migration to the American Southwest", and argues that "a large space/time gap in the period 1200-1400 AD" in the record should prompt a new look at old collections for Proto-Apachean artifacts. The gap is a reason to search, not an arrival date. One author's argument.
   - [Seymour 2012] (full text, read 2026-10-29): "The earliest known dates (A.D. 1300s and 1400s) for ancestral Athabascans are found along the Rio Grande valley in the Southwest and in the bordering mountains of southern New Mexico and Arizona." She adds that the earliest dated sites are few but have "high quality direct dates". Upgraded from WEAK, which recorded only that the first pass read a search summary. Full text read 2026-10-29 at https://camnnation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Gateways-for-Athabascan-Migration-to-the-American-Southwest-Seymour-Deni-J.pdf.
3. The migrants are hard to see in the ground, which is part of why scholars disagree.
   - [Gordon]: Dene groups often adopted local toolkits, leaving little distinctive material, and tree-ring dates are unreliable where hogans used older wood.
4. The route is also debated: across the Plains, down the Rocky Mountain foothills, or through the Intermountain West.
   - [Gordon]: Sapir's Plains route was favored for decades, later work shifted attention to an Intermountain route, and the author argues the Plains route has never been ruled out.

Draft card: "The ancestors of the Navajo and Apache spoke Dene languages from the far north and made their way to the American Southwest, probably after about AD 1450, perhaps earlier. Researchers still disagree about when, and which way they came." (Second check: "sometime between about AD 1200 and 1500" replaced.)

## Uto-Aztecan

- id: `uto-aztecan`
- kind: family
- date range: "No consensus. Merrill et al. (2009) put the break-up about 6900 cal BCE (about 8,900 years ago); the older view, as Hill (2001) describes it, put the speakers about 5,000 years ago; Hill's own farming model ties the spread to 4500-3000 BP." These are different proposals from different dates, not one range of estimates. (Second check: "Roughly 9,000 to 5,000 years ago" read as one range of current estimates; the retired `uto-aztecan` stop's ~9,000–5,000 years ago had the same problem.)
- era year: -5000 (editorial midpoint of proposals, WEAK)
- location: (30.7, -110.9) Glottolog's center of the family, near the Arizona-Sonora border, radius ~1500 km. Two candidate homelands to show, if the page can show both: (39.0, -117.0) west-central Great Basin and (23.0, -104.5) northwest Mexico.
- location uncertainty: High on purpose. The homeland is the classic open question for this family. Do not pin it to a single dot.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (40.0, -115.0) Great Basin (Numic: Shoshone, Ute, Comanche)
  - (35.8, -110.5) Hopi
  - (19.5, -99.0) central Mexico (Nahuatl)
- scholars split? Yes, sharply, on homeland and on what drove the spread. (1) Great Basin hunter-gatherers, with maize adopted later by neighbors; or (2) maize farmers in northwest Mexico moving north.

Claims:

1. Uto-Aztecan is a large family running from the Great Basin to northern Central America. It includes Shoshone, Hopi and Nahuatl.
   - [EBSCO Uto-Aztecan]: speakers have lived "from central Idaho in the north to northern Central America in the south"; eight branches.
   - [Glottolog]: 69 entries in the family (extinct included); the family includes Hopi, Shoshoni and Classical Nahuatl.
2. One view puts the homeland in the Great Basin, with a break-up around 6900 cal BCE, and says maize spread by neighbors passing it on.
   - [Merrill 2009]: the abstract gives "a Great Basin homeland for the PUA, the breakup of this speech community into northern and southern divisions approximately 6900 cal. B.C."; maize reached the US Southwest by about 2100 cal BCE "via group-to-group diffusion", not by migrating farmers. The west-central Great Basin location, the 9,700 BCE start of the first pass's range and the southeastward move come from the full text, which could not be re-opened on 2026-10-29 (PMC returned a captcha). NOT RE-VERIFIED.
3. The other view puts the homeland among maize farmers in Mesoamerica, with farming driving a northward expansion.
   - [Hill 2001] (abstract, read 2026-10-29): earlier authorities held that the speakers "were foragers who lived in upland regions of Arizona, New Mexico, and the adjacent areas of the Mexican states of Sonora and Chihuahua about 5,000 years ago"; new lexical evidence suggests instead that they "were maize cultivators", that the community "was probably located in Mesoamerica and spread northward into the present range because of demographic pressure associated with cultivation", and that the spread should match the northward spread of maize "between 4500 and 3000 B.P."
   - [Merrill 2009]: describes Hill's position as a community of maize farmers in what is now Jalisco, Nayarit, Durango, Zacatecas and Aguascalientes. The list of states is Merrill's (a paper that rejects her view); Hill's abstract says only "Mesoamerica".
4. DNA does not settle the question.
   - [Kemp 2010]: Y-chromosome variation correlates with language distance but mtDNA does not; if Uto-Aztecan farmers carried maize north, the move "was possibly biased to males", but the authors call a migration from Mesoamerica "unlikely", and also find a local population expansion in the Southwest about 2,100 years ago.

Draft card: "Uto-Aztecan languages, including Shoshone, Hopi and Nahuatl, stretch from the western United States to central Mexico. Scholars disagree whether the family began with hunters in the Great Basin or with maize farmers farther south in Mexico."

## Nahuatl and the Aztec Empire

- id: `nahuatl-aztec-empire`
- kind: language
- date range: "AD 1325 to 1521"
- era year: 1428
- location: (19.43, -99.13) Tenochtitlan, today's Mexico City, radius ~250 km (editorial: the Valley of Mexico core; no source gives a radius).
- location uncertainty: Low for the city. The reach of Nahuatl-speaking rule is not mapped in the sources used here, so the page should not draw arrows without a further source.
- spread to: none sourced.
- scholars split? Only on details. 1325 is the conventional founding date. 1428 is the standard date for the Triple Alliance.

Claims:

1. The Mexica spoke Nahuatl, the same language as the earlier Toltec civilization, and founded their island capital Tenochtitlan around 1325.
   - [NatGeo Aztec]: "They spoke Nahuatl, the same tongue of the mighty Toltec civilization who dominated the region between the 10th and 12th centuries"; "Around 1325, the Mexica founded Tenochtitlan and made it their capital" (re-read 2026-10-29).
   - [NatGeo Education]: "Archaeologists date the founding of Tenochtitlan to 1325 C.E."
   - [Jordan]: UC San Diego course site: the Mexica founded Tenochtitlan in 1325.
2. In 1428 Tenochtitlan joined with Texcoco and Tlacopan in the Triple Alliance, the core of what is called the Aztec Empire.
   - [NatGeo Education]: in 1428 the Mexica allied with Texcoco and Tlacopan; together they "won control of the region and collected tribute from conquered states".
   - [Jordan]: the alliance was never a group of equals; Tenochtitlan was the dominant player.
3. Nahuatl is still the most-spoken Indigenous language in Mexico.
   - [Indigenous Mexico 2020]: reports 1,651,958 Nahuatl speakers aged 3 and over in the 2020 census, 22.4% of Indigenous-language speakers, ahead of Maya (774,755). WEAK: the page cites INEGI's 2020 census tables but the investigator could not open INEGI's own table; an INEGI 2024 press release was found but not readable.
4. Nahuatl belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family (see previous stop).
   - [Glottolog]: Classical Nahuatl is listed in Uto-Aztecan.

Draft card: "Nahuatl was the language of the Mexica, who founded Tenochtitlan around 1325 and, with two allied cities, built what we call the Aztec Empire. Roughly 1.6 million people still speak it today."

## Mayan languages

- id: `mayan`
- kind: family
- date range: "Proto-Mayan about 2200 BCE by one widely used estimate; other estimates run from about 4000 BCE to about 200 BCE"
- era year: -2200
- location: (15.8, -91.4) near Soloma, Huehuetenango, in the Cuchumatanes highlands of Guatemala, radius ~200 km.
- location uncertainty: Moderate. The highland homeland is the dominant view, and Kaufman narrowed it to the Soloma area, but the arguments for that exact spot are described as not conclusive, and some scholars prefer a lowland homeland.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (21.5, -98.5) Huastec area, Gulf Coast of Mexico (the first branch to leave, on Kaufman's tree)
  - (18.8, -89.0) Yucatán lowlands
  - (16.7, -92.6) Chiapas highlands
- scholars split? Yes, on the date (a factor of several) and mildly on the homeland (highlands vs lowlands).

Claims:

1. About 30 Mayan languages exist today, spoken by well over 5 million people in Guatemala, Mexico, Belize and Honduras. Re-checked 2026-10-29 in public copies of both full texts (see the bibliography).
   - [Law 2013]: "between 29 and 31 contemporary languages spoken collectively by more than 5 million people".
   - [Bennett 2015]: "over 6 million people"; "about 40% of Guatemalans speak a Mayan language natively" on official census estimates, likely "50–60% or higher" in reality.
   - [Glottolog]: lists 34 entries (extinct included).
2. The usual view puts the ancestral language in the Guatemalan highlands, near Soloma, before it split.
   - [Law 2013] (full text): "by far the dominant hypothesis" places Proto-Mayan in the southern highlands of Guatemala; Kaufman (1976) narrowed it to Soloma. Of two of Kaufman's supporting arguments, Law writes: "Neither of these arguments is probative, however." Law also notes that the earliest maize and pottery are lowland, which has led some to prefer a lowland homeland.
3. The "about 2200 BCE" date is a glottochronology estimate and is contested. The retired `mayan` stop's ~2200 BCE rested on this.
   - [Law 2013] (full text): Kaufman's estimates "place the initial break-up of proto-Mayan ... at 2200 B.C.", still widely used by archaeologists; a computational model gives a time depth of 6,000 to 6,500 years (Atkinson 2006), and another gives about 2,200 years ago (Holman 2011). "No published work has evaluated these hypotheses for Mayan languages specifically."
4. Proto-Mayan speakers farmed maize, beans and squash and kept dogs and turkeys.
   - [Law 2013] (full text): reconstructed vocabulary shows that its speakers had "well developed agricultural practices, cultivated maize, beans, squash, chiles and other plants and had domesticated dogs and turkeys".

Draft card: "Mayan languages grew from a single ancestor that most scholars place in the highlands of Guatemala several thousand years ago. About 30 survive today, spoken by millions of people. The exact date is debated."

## Classic Maya writing

- id: `classic-maya-writing`
- kind: language
- date range: "AD 250 to 900 (Classic period); earliest Maya writing about 300 to 200 BCE"
- era year: 600
- location: (17.2, -89.6) Tikal area, northern Guatemala, radius ~300 km (lowland Maya heartland).
- location uncertainty: Moderate. The script was used across a wide lowland region; Tikal is a representative city, not the origin.
- spread to: none.
- scholars split? Only on how far back the script goes and how the Classic written language relates to the spoken languages. Most hieroglyphic texts are in a Ch'olan language.

Claims:

1. Maya writing is older than the Classic period. The earliest securely dated examples are from San Bartolo, Guatemala, about 300 to 200 BCE.
   - [Saturno 2006]: writing at San Bartolo with calibrated radiocarbon dates of 200 to 300 BCE, "centuries earlier than previously thought".
   - [Stuart 2022]: fragments sealed in an early building phase, 300 to 200 BCE, include the earliest known Maya calendar record.
2. During the Classic period (AD 250 to 900), the lowland Maya used a fully developed hieroglyphic writing system.
   - [Kelly 2017]: "During the Classic period (AD 250-900), the Maya ... institutionalized a hieroglyphic writing system."
3. The language written in most Classic inscriptions is a Ch'olan language, not the everyday speech of every city.
   - [Law 2013] (full text): "some 20,000 terse hieroglyphic inscriptions" survive; "the vast majority" are written in a Ch'olan language, and the language of the hieroglyphs was a single unified one even though the Maya spoke many languages.
   - [Kelly 2017]: the glyphs record a largely uniform written language despite the spoken variety.

Draft card: "Classic Maya kings carved texts in a hieroglyphic script between about AD 250 and 900. Most of those texts are written in a Ch'olan language, even though the Maya spoke many languages."

## Quechua before the Incas

- id: `quechuan-origins`
- kind: family
- date range: "Quechuan branches spread over roughly the last 2,000 years; the first big spread may have come with Wari (about AD 600 to 1000), but this is debated"
- era year: 500 (editorial; no source gives a single date, WEAK)
- location: (-11.5, -76.5) central Peru (central coast and highlands), radius ~250 km.
- location uncertainty: Moderate. Specialists put the homeland in central Peru but disagree whether on the central coast (Torero) or in the central highlands (Cerrón-Palomino).
- spread to (approximate):
  - (-9.4, -77.8) Ancash (north)
  - (-13.8, -74.3) Ayacucho (south)
  - (-14.1, -71.8) Cusco area
- scholars split? Yes. Where in central Peru, what drove the spread (Wari, Chavín, other), and the date. The point that the Incas did not start Quechua is broadly accepted among linguists.

Claims:

1. "Quechua" is a family of related languages, not one language. Its branches split within roughly the last 2,000 years.
   - [Heggarty and Beresford-Jones 2010]: "Quechua is not a single language at all, but a language family".
   - [Adelaar 2017]: Aymaran and Quechuan are two families "with an internal diversification that probably does not exceed 2,000 years".
2. The homeland was in central Peru. Specialists differ on whether it was the central coast or the central highlands.
   - [Heggarty and Beresford-Jones 2010]: Torero places it on the central coast, Cerrón-Palomino "now prefers to set it inland, in the central highlands"; an earlier far-back stage in the Norte Chico area is "generally regarded as extremely speculative".
   - [Adelaar 2017]: early contact between Quechuan and Aymaran was "most likely situated in the Andes of Central Peru".
3. Quechua had spread widely across central and southern Peru many centuries before the Incas rose.
   - [Heggarty and Beresford-Jones 2010]: it has "long been clear to historical linguists that Quechua had already spread widely across central and southern Peru many centuries before the Incas first rose out of obscurity".
   - [Heggarty 2007]: sets the Inca Empire and the Quechua family side by side and finds an assumed link between them "nothing but a popular myth".
4. What drove the first spread is open. One proposal says Wari; another view is cautious about tying a state to one language.
   - [Beresford-Jones and Heggarty 2012]: the Incas are "too late for the time-depth of either family"; Wari emerges as "the most plausible candidate" for Quechua's first major expansion.
   - [Adelaar 2017]: urges care in giving Wari "an exclusive linguistic identity"; "a multilingual situation would be in the line of expectation".

Draft card: "Quechua did not begin with the Incas. It grew out of central Peru and was already spreading across the Andes centuries before Cusco became a capital. Scholars still debate what carried it."

## Quechua as the language of the Inca Empire

- id: `quechua-inca-empire`
- kind: language
- date range: "About AD 1400s to 1532"
- era year: 1450
- location: (-13.53, -71.97) Cusco, radius ~150 km.
- location uncertainty: Low for the capital. Quechua in the regions the Incas conquered came in different ways, so arrows are approximate.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (-19.0, -65.3) southern Bolivia (Sucre area)
  - (-26.8, -65.2) northwest Argentina (the authors say deriving Argentine Quechua from Inca-era Cuzco Quechua is "not quite so straightforward"; see claim 2)
  - (-0.2, -78.5) highland Ecuador (Quito area)
- scholars split? Mildly, on how much of today's Quechua map is Inca-era. Southern Bolivia is clearly from Cuzco Quechua. Ecuador is more complicated.

Claims:

1. The Incas made Quechua their official language and unified the Central Andes in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries.
   - [Heggarty and Beresford-Jones 2010]: "the Incas themselves did indeed promote Quechua as their 'official language' of Empire."
   - [Adelaar 2017]: "The ultimate unification of the Central Andean region was achieved by the Incas of Cuzco in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries."
2. Quechua in southern Bolivia comes from Cuzco Quechua of the Inca period. Argentina's is less clear, and Ecuador's Quechua was not simply moved from Cuzco.
   - [Heggarty and Beresford-Jones 2010]: southern Bolivian forms "can be derived directly from that of Cuzco at the time of Inca imperial expansion", but "this is not quite so straightforward even for the far-flung Quechua of Argentina"; the Quechua of Ecuador "was certainly not transplanted from Cuzco", though Inca policy may have moved speakers there. Elsewhere the same paper groups north-west Argentina with southern Bolivia among Quechua's recent (Inca and colonial) expansions.
3. Cusco itself is full of older, non-Quechua names.
   - [Heggarty and Beresford-Jones 2010]: the Inca heartland has place names that look Aymara, such as the river Vilcanota and Cuzco itself; Spanish chroniclers describe a "secret language" of the Inca nobility whose verses "clearly betray their non-Quechua origins".
4. Quechua is today the most widely spoken Indigenous language family of the Americas, with a total usually given as roughly 8 to 12 million.
   - [Illinois Quechua guide]: gives 8-12 million speakers, mainly in Peru, Bolivia and Ecuador, and calls Quechua the "Most spoken Amerindian language in the Americas". WEAK for the total: library guide, estimates vary and no census total across countries was found.
   - [INEI 2017]: in Peru's 2017 census, 3,735,682 people aged 5 and over (13.9%) said they learned Quechua in childhood.

Draft card: "The Incas made Quechua the language of their empire, carrying it to places like southern Bolivia. But Quechua was already old and widespread, and even Cusco's own place names show older languages underneath."

## Tupian

- id: `tupian`
- kind: family
- date range: "Family origin about 3,000 years ago by one estimate (older by others); no secure date"
- era year: -1000 (editorial, WEAK)
- location: (-11.0, -62.5) Rondônia / Madeira-Guaporé region, southwestern Amazon, radius ~300 km.
- location uncertainty: Moderate. Linguistic variety and genetics point to the Madeira-Guaporé region, but some archaeologists prefer the Madeira-Amazon junction farther north. The Tupi-Guarani branch started farther east (next stop).
- spread to (approximate):
  - (-9.5, -54.5) Tapajós-Xingu uplands (where Tupi-Guarani began)
- scholars split? Yes, on homeland (Madeira-Guaporé vs the Madeira-Amazon junction) and on dates.

Claims:

1. The Tupian family has many languages, spread widely across lowland South America.
   - [Walker 2012]: "a widely-dispersed language family in lowland South America".
   - [Glottolog]: 70 entries in Tupian (extinct included).
2. Language variety and genetics place the homeland in the Madeira-Guaporé region (Rondônia).
   - [Santos 2015]: reviewing genetic data, concludes the Madeira-Guaporé region was the homeland; it notes Tupian linguistic variety is highest there and most Tupí languages are spoken in Rondônia, citing Rodrigues (1964), Migliazza (1982) and Urban (1996, 1998).
   - [Walker 2012]: a vocabulary-based family tree "supports a Tupi homeland in west-central Brazil".
3. Some archaeological work points instead to the junction of the Madeira and Amazon rivers.
   - [Santos 2015]: notes archaeologists such as Heckenberger et al. (1998) and Noelli (1998) favor that junction.
4. Estimated dates for the family's origin are only a few thousand years back.
   - [Santos 2015]: "linguistic and genetic data point to the origin of the Tupí family 'between 2800 and 3000 years ago'", citing Urban (1998) and Amorim et al. (2013).
   - [Gerardi 2023]: in its tree, the Mawé branch split from the rest about 3,300 years ago.

Draft card: "The Tupian languages began in the southwestern Amazon, in what is now Rondônia in Brazil. From there, over a few thousand years, they spread across much of lowland South America."

## Tupi-Guarani

- id: `tupi-guarani`
- kind: branch
- date range: "Began about 2,500 years ago; spread from about 1,700 years ago"
- era year: 0 (editorial midpoint, WEAK)
- location: (-9.5, -54.5) upper Tapajós-Xingu, radius ~300 km.
- location uncertainty: Moderate. A computer model places the start there, but other proposals place it farther southwest or at the lower Xingu.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (-22.9, -43.2) Atlantic coast (the Tupinambá region)
  - (-25.6, -57.1) Paraguay River (Guaraní)
  - (-17.8, -63.2) lowland Bolivia (Guarayo, Chiriguano)
- scholars split? Yes, on dates. Computer models of word lists point to about 1,700 years ago for the big spread. Some archaeologists date the pottery tradition earlier, and some southern-Brazil dates conflict with the model.

Claims:

1. The Tupi-Guarani branch began about 2,500 years ago in the upper Tapajós-Xingu area and spread quickly after about 1,700 years ago.
   - [Gerardi 2023]: dated tree and map model: "originated around 2500 BP in the upper Tapajós-Xingu basins", with a split into southern and northern groups from about 1,750 years ago; spreading from about 1,700 years ago.
2. Archaeology shows a pottery-and-farming package spreading across eastern South America, but the dates are uneven and some are disputed.
   - [Gerardi 2023]: the earliest reliable date in the Paraná basin is about 2,010 years ago; the coast may go back about 1,880 years, with most dates later; a few older coastal dates (to about 2,900 years) are "controversial" and "predate the TG expansion out of the Amazon by any estimate". The authors say matching language and archaeology is hard.
3. Guaraní is the biggest living language in the branch, with over 6 million speakers.
   - [Gerardi 2023]: about 40 living languages, from under 100 speakers to "over 6 million (Paraguayan Guaraní)", citing Ethnologue. WEAK: relayed from Ethnologue, which the investigator could not open.
4. Guaraní is an official language of Paraguay, alongside Spanish.
   - [Paraguay Constitution]: Article 140: "Castilian and Guarani are official languages."

Draft card: "Tupi-Guarani languages started in the Amazon basin about 2,500 years ago and spread along rivers and down to the Atlantic coast. Guaraní, one of them, is an official language of Paraguay today."

## Arawakan

- id: `arawakan`
- kind: family
- date range: "Origin undated; expansion into the Caribbean began about 2,500 years ago"
- era year: -1500 (editorial placeholder, must predate the Caribbean move; WEAK)
- location: (-8.0, -67.0) Purus River area, western Amazon, radius ~600 km. Traditional alternative: (1.0, -68.0) northwest Amazon.
- location uncertainty: High. The homeland is a live debate.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (18.0, -70.0) Caribbean islands
  - (-12.0, -72.0) Andean foothills
  - (-20.5, -55.5) southern edge of the family's range (Mato Grosso do Sul)
- scholars split? Yes, on the homeland: northwest Amazon (where Arawakan languages are most varied) vs western Amazon (a statistical model) vs an Atlantic seaboard possibility.

Claims:

1. Arawakan is one of the most widely scattered language families in the Americas, from the Antilles to Argentina.
   - [Walker and Ribeiro 2011]: "one of the most widely dispersed language families in the Americas, scattered from the Antilles to Argentina"; its societies occupy rainforest, Andean foothills, Caribbean coasts and islands, dry forests and savannahs.
   - [Glottolog]: 77 entries (extinct included).
2. The homeland has often been placed in the northwest Amazon, but one model argues otherwise.
   - [Walker and Ribeiro 2011]: "It has been suggested that Northwest Amazonia is the Arawak homeland based on the large number of diverse languages in the region"; their tree and map model for 60 languages and dialects "exclude Northwest Amazonia", favoring western Amazonia, with the Atlantic seaboard a second, weaker option.
3. The family spread with cultural traits, not language alone, and one route reached the Caribbean by way of the Guianas.
   - [Walker and Ribeiro 2011]: the expansion "carried with it not only language but also a number of cultural traits that contrast Arawak societies with other lowland cultures"; the authors call a route up the Rio Branco toward the Guianas and then the Caribbean a likely scenario, not a firm result.
4. "Most widespread in South America" is not a claim any source checked states directly; say "one of the most widely scattered".

Draft card: "Arawakan languages were once spoken from the Caribbean islands to the foothills of the Andes, which makes them one of the most widely scattered families in the Americas. Where the family began is still debated."

## Arawakan speakers reach the Caribbean (Taíno)

- id: `arawakan-taino-caribbean`
- kind: migration
- date range: "From about 2,500 years ago; Taíno across the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas by 1492"
- era year: -500
- location: (8.6, -61.5) lower Orinoco / northern South America coast, radius ~500 km.
- location uncertainty: High. Genetic and language evidence says "northern South America, Amazon and Orinoco basins". The exact departure area and the route are debated.
- spread to (approximate):
  - (18.3, -66.5) Puerto Rico
  - (19.0, -70.7) Hispaniola
  - (21.0, -77.5) Cuba
  - (24.0, -76.0) Bahamas (Lucayans)
- scholars split? Mildly. Most agree on a South American origin for the Taíno ancestors. How early the first arrivals were, which route they took, and how many earlier waves existed are debated.

Claims:

1. The ancestors of the Taíno are thought to have been Arawakan speakers who came into the Caribbean from South America from as early as about 2,500 years ago. The Bahamas were settled later.
   - [Schroeder 2018]: "The ancestors of the Taino are thought to have been Arawakan speakers who entered the Caribbean from South America, starting as early as 2,500 y cal BP"; the Bahamas were not settled for about 1,000 years more, as part of the Ostionoid expansion starting around 1,400 cal BP.
2. DNA from a Lucayan woman of about 1,000 years ago is closest to living Arawakan speakers of northern South America.
   - [Schroeder 2018]: her genome is "most closely related to present-day Arawakan speakers from northern South America" (Palikur and other Arawakan speakers of the Amazon and Orinoco basins). The authors note that opinions on the origin differ, with some scholars preferring the Colombian Andes.
3. The Taíno were not the first people in the Caribbean. A later wave from South America arrived with almost no mixing with earlier settlers.
   - [Nägele 2020]: DNA from 93 ancient islanders shows at least three separate dispersals into the Caribbean, with a later expansion from South America and "almost no evidence of admixture" with earlier settlers.
4. Language trees agree that the Caribbean languages belong with the main Arawakan family.
   - [Walker and Ribeiro 2011]: a clade of Marawa and Waraicu groups with the Circum-Caribbean languages (posterior probability 0.93).
   - [Glottolog]: lists Taíno under Arawakan.
5. Taíno itself is poorly documented. WEAK: only the existence of a critical review of the main book on it, Adelaar's 2009 review of Granberry and Vescelius (2004) in IJAL, was confirmed; its argument was not read. Do not claim detail on Taíno structure.

Draft card: "Arawakan speakers moved from northern South America into the Caribbean islands starting around 2,500 years ago. By 1492 their descendants, the Taíno, lived across the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas."
