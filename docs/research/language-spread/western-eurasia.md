# How languages spread: western and central Eurasia

Research notes for the western and central Eurasia stops of the "How languages spread" page. Claims were checked against the abstract, publisher description or fetched text of each source (see "How sources were checked" at the end). Items marked WEAK rest on one secondary source or on text I could not open, and should be dropped or softened. Wikipedia was used only to find leads and is not cited.

Conventions: years are negative for BCE. Coordinates are decimal degrees (lat, lon). "Era year" is the single year for the era indicator. Citation keys such as [Haak 2015] point to the Bibliography.

Stops covered:

1. indo-european (family, with the homeland debate)
2. anatolian-hittite (branch)
3. indo-iranian (branch)
4. sanskrit-rigveda (language)
5. latin-romance (language)
6. germanic-english (branch)
7. uralic (family)
8. hungarian (language)
9. turkic (family)
10. turkish-anatolia (language)
11. arabic (language)
12. basque (language, isolate)

Note on scope: Kartvelian, Celtic and Slavic were not given stops. The Germanic stop was picked because English descends from it.

## Indo-European: where did it start?

- id: `indo-european`
- kind: family
- date range: "Roughly 6,000 to 8,000 years ago for the common ancestor, depending on the model. Spread across Europe and into Asia from about 5,000 years ago."
- era year: -3300
- location: (47.0, 42.0), radius ~700 km. This is the Pontic-Caspian steppe (north of the Black and Caspian Seas), the leading candidate. Alternative points to show only if the page wants to draw the debate: south of the Caucasus / eastern Anatolia (39.5, 43.0), and the lower Volga where the 2025 paper places the northern end of its Caucasus-Lower Volga group (Berezhnovka, about 50.7, 46.0). The Anatolian farming candidate is central Anatolia (38.0, 33.0).
- location uncertainty: High. The location is the open question of the stop. Draw a wide soft region, not a pin, and say so on the card.
- spread to (arrows): central Europe (50.0, 14.0); the steppe and Central Asia east of the Urals (52.5, 60.0); Anatolia (39.0, 35.0) and Iran/South Asia (33.0, 65.0) are covered in later stops.
- scholars split? Yes, on where and when. The balance of the evidence in 2025: most archaeologists, ancient-DNA researchers and many historical linguists favour a steppe (or steppe-and-Caucasus) origin; a group of computational linguists favours an older origin south of the Caucasus or in Anatolia. See claim 4 for who sits where.

Claims:

1. Indo-European is the family that includes most languages of Europe plus Persian, Hindi and many more. Its members all descend from one ancestor language, spoken before writing.
   - [Anthony and Ringe 2015]: describes "the Indo-European homeland" as a question of where this single ancestor was spoken, and argues for the Pontic-Caspian steppes around 4,000 BCE.
   - [Bouckaert 2012]: opens by describing the two competing views of where "the Indo-European language family" began.
2. Steppe view: the ancestor was spoken by herders on the Pontic-Caspian steppe around 6,000 years ago, and spread with the Yamnaya people (and their kin) from about 3300 BCE. Two kinds of evidence support this.
   - [Haak 2015]: ancient DNA shows the Yamnaya herders of about 5,000 to 6,000 years ago had both eastern European hunter-gatherer and Near Eastern ancestry, and that Corded Ware people in Germany about 4,500 years ago traced about 75% of their ancestry to the Yamnaya. The authors conclude this supports "a steppe origin of at least some of the Indo-European languages of Europe."
   - [Anthony and Ringe 2015] and [Anthony 2007]: the reconstructed ancestor language has words for wheeled vehicles and wagons, which fits a people who lived in the same period as the first wagons, and the steppe fits the archaeology of horses, wagons and herding.
3. Anatolian farming view: the ancestor spread from Anatolia (modern Turkey) with the first farmers, 8,000 to 9,500 years ago.
   - [Bouckaert 2012]: an analysis of basic vocabulary from 103 ancient and modern languages found "decisive support for an Anatolian origin over a steppe origin", with timing of 8,000 to 9,500 years ago.
   - [Zimmer 2025] (journalism, secondary): the archaeologist Colin Renfrew argued in 1987 that early farmers in Anatolia spread Indo-European.
4. Newer middle views: a 2023 language study and a 2025 DNA study each moved the picture toward the Caucasus. They do not agree with each other about what that means.
   - [Heggarty 2023]: a new vocabulary dataset gives a root age of about 8,120 years ago, which is "not consistent with the Steppe hypothesis" but "does not rule out an initial homeland south of the Caucasus, with a subsequent branch northward onto the steppe and then across Europe." The authors call this a hybrid hypothesis.
   - [Lazaridis 2025]: ancient DNA from 435 people shows that a group living between the Caucasus and the lower Volga river contributed "around four-fifths" of the ancestry of the Yamnaya and at least one-tenth of the ancestry of Bronze Age central Anatolians, who spoke Hittite. The authors propose that the common ancestor of Anatolian and all other Indo-European languages (which they call "Proto-Indo-Anatolian") was spoken by that group between 4400 and 4000 BCE, and that the Yamnaya spoke the ancestor of the rest.
5. How the experts have reacted.
   - [Zimmer 2025]: the Leiden linguist Guus Kroonen, not an author, called the 2025 scenario "very intelligent" and "difficult to criticize". Paul Heggarty (lead author of the 2023 hybrid paper) welcomed the DNA data but rejected the paper's linguistic conclusions. Mait Metspalu of Tartu praised the sampling and said genes alone do not show which language people spoke.
   - [Chang 2015]: an ancestry-constrained language-tree analysis "strongly supports the steppe hypothesis", so the vocabulary-tree method itself gives different answers depending on how it is set up.
   - [Damgaard 2018 Science]: no steppe-related ancestry in Bronze Age Anatolia when Indo-European languages are attested there. This was a main argument against the steppe view for Anatolian; [Lazaridis 2025] answers it with an earlier Caucasus source.

Where scholars are closely split: the steppe/Caucasus-edge view (Anthony, Reich, Lazaridis, many archaeologists and geneticists, and the vocabulary-tree analysis of Chang et al.) against the older "south of the Caucasus" or "Anatolia" views (Renfrew, Bouckaert et al., Heggarty et al.). The two camps now overlap more than they did in 2012: both the 2023 and 2025 papers put the earliest speakers near the Caucasus, and differ mainly on whether the steppe was the first or second stop and on how old the family is (about 6,000 vs about 8,000 years). Linguists and geneticists also disagree on how far DNA can say anything about language.

Draft card: "Most languages of Europe, and Persian, Hindi and many more, trace back to one ancestor language. Where it was spoken is still argued: on the grasslands north of the Black Sea, or further south near the Caucasus. Either way, it spread widely over the last 5,000 years."

## Hittite and the Anatolian branch

- id: `anatolian-hittite`
- kind: branch
- date range: "Names and words from about 1900 BCE; full Hittite texts from the 1600s BCE; the branch died out by about 1000 BCE to the early centuries CE"
- era year: -1700
- location: (40.0, 34.6), radius ~250 km. Hattusa (modern Boğazkale), the Hittite capital, is at about (40.02, 34.61). The earliest traces are at Kanesh (Kültepe) at about (38.85, 35.63).
- location uncertainty: Low for the centre of the Hittite kingdom (central Anatolia). The Anatolian languages were spoken across Anatolia, so the radius is for the region. Where the speakers came from before that is the open question in the previous stop.
- spread to (arrows): none needed. Optionally an arrow into Anatolia from the east/north-east (Caucasus) at about (41.0, 44.0) to show the 2025 proposal, which should be labelled as a proposal.
- scholars split? Yes, on how the Anatolian branch reached Anatolia (see indo-european). Not split on the fact that Hittite is the oldest written Indo-European language.

Claims:

1. Hittite is the oldest Indo-European language we have in writing, and it was spoken only in Anatolia. The first traces are personal names and some words in the business letters of Assyrian merchants, about 1900 BCE. Full texts in Hittite come later.
   - [Kloekhorst 2019]: a book-length study titled "Kanišite Hittite: The Earliest Attested Record of Indo-European", based on personal names in Old Assyrian merchant documents from Kanesh (Kültepe). The publisher and reviews describe the period as the merchant-colony ("kārum") age, about 1970 to 1710 BCE.
   - [Zimmer 2025] (secondary): "The oldest Indo-European writing, about 3,700 years old, is in Hittite, which was spoken only in Anatolia."
   - [Lazaridis 2025]: states Hittite was attested in central Anatolia after 2000 BCE.
2. Hittite is not a descendant of Latin, Greek or Sanskrit. It is a separate branch, probably the first to split from the rest of the family (which the 2025 paper's authors call "Indo-Anatolian" for the whole family).
   - [Lazaridis 2025]: explains that the traditional view treats Anatolian as the first split of the family.
   - [Kloekhorst 2019]: title and description treat Kanišite Hittite as a record of early Indo-European.
3. Ancient DNA from Anatolia did not show a large steppe-herder component in the Bronze Age Hittite lands, which is one reason Anatolia is used to argue against a steppe origin.
   - [Damgaard 2018 Science]: "We find no evidence of steppe ancestry in Bronze Age Anatolia from when Indo-European languages are attested there."
   - [Lazaridis 2025]: argues that a Caucasus-Lower Volga group entered Anatolia, "probably from the east", and gave central Anatolians at least a tenth of their ancestry.

Where scholars are closely split: how the speakers of Anatolian got to Anatolia. One view is that they were already there as early farmers (Anatolian farming hypothesis). The other is that they came from the Caucasus-steppe region around 4000 BCE or later. Both agree on the oldest written evidence.

Draft card: "The oldest Indo-European language we can read is Hittite, written in Turkey about 3,700 years ago. It belongs to a branch of its own and died out long ago."

WEAK / dropped: the Anitta text as "oldest Hittite-language text, 1700s BCE" appeared only in search summaries that cite mirrored encyclopedia text, so I did not use it. The "1,700s-1,600s BCE" start date in the brief is for full Hittite texts; names and words from Kanesh go back to about 1900 BCE.

## Indo-Iranian

- id: `indo-iranian`
- kind: branch
- date range: "Spoken on the steppe east of the Urals about 4,000 years ago; reaches Iran, Mesopotamia and South Asia over the following centuries"
- era year: -2000
- location: (52.6, 59.5), radius ~500 km. This is the Sintashta-Arkaim area south of the Urals, where horse-drawn chariots are first found.
- location uncertainty: Medium-high. A single scholar's reconstruction (Parpola 2022) ties it to the Sintashta culture. Others place the Indo-Iranian homeland in the wider Central Asian steppe or the Andronovo world.
- spread to (arrows): Iranian plateau (33.0, 52.0); the Punjab and the upper Indus (31.0, 73.0); northern Mesopotamia/Syria (36.5, 40.0) for the Mitanni.
- scholars split? On the details, yes. That Indo-Aryan and Iranian split from one ancestor and that the Indo-Aryans reached South Asia in the 2nd millennium BCE is the mainstream view. A minority position within India holds Indo-Aryan is native to South Asia ([Bryant 2001] describes the debate; I did not verify its details).

Claims:

1. Sanskrit and Persian are close cousins. Both come from an ancestor often called Indo-Iranian, which broke into the Indo-Aryan group (Sanskrit and its descendants) and the Iranian group (Persian, Avestan and others).
   - [Narasimhan 2019]: ancient DNA shows people with steppe herder ancestry spread via Central Asia into South Asia from about 4,000 years ago. The authors say that this "likely spread the distinctive features shared between Indo-Iranian and Balto-Slavic languages."
   - [Burrow 1973]: the Indo-European traces in the Near East (the Mitanni) are Indo-Aryan, not Iranian.
2. Chariots are part of the story. The earliest horse-drawn chariots in the archaeological record are in the Sintashta culture (about 2000-1900 BCE), and Indo-Iranian had words for chariot parts.
   - [Parpola 2022]: argues the Sintashta culture matches Proto-Indo-Iranian chariot vocabulary, and places the pre-Indo-Iranians in the Abashevo culture. This is one scholar's argument, but the chariot dating is widely used. Mark as WEAK on the exact culture match.
   - [Anthony 2007]: describes the spoked wheel and the chariot as steppe innovations.
3. The Indus Valley civilization (about 2600-1900 BCE) was not a steppe-herder society. A person from one of its cities has almost no steppe ancestry, which fits Indo-Aryan arriving afterwards.
   - [Shinde 2019]: a genome from the Indus Valley Civilization "had little if any Steppe pastoralist-derived ancestry".
   - [Narasimhan 2019]: steppe ancestry in South Asia arrives after the Indus Valley Civilization's decline.
4. A branch of Indo-Aryan turned up far to the west, in northern Syria and Mesopotamia, about 1400 BCE.
   - [Burrow 1973]: four gods named in a Hittite-Mitanni treaty are identifiable as Vedic deities: Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the Nasatyas.
   - [Thieme 1960]: titled "The 'Aryan' Gods of the Mitanni Treaties"; the argument that the divine names are Indic, not Iranian, is cited in the secondary literature, but I only confirmed the title, not the text.

Where scholars are closely split: how the Indo-Iranians got from the steppe to Iran and South Asia (one wave or several, and through which Central Asian cultures). The date range for the Indo-Aryan arrival is also broad (about 2000-1200 BCE).

Draft card: "Sanskrit, Persian, Hindi and Kurdish share one ancestor. Its speakers lived on the steppe east of the Urals about 4,000 years ago, and groups moved south to Iran and India over the next centuries."

## Sanskrit and the Rigveda

- id: `sanskrit-rigveda`
- kind: language
- date range: "Composed about 1500 to 1000 BCE; kept by memory for more than 2,000 years before being written"
- era year: -1300
- location: (31.0, 73.0), radius ~400 km. The Punjab and the upper Indus plains.
- location uncertainty: Medium. The hymns mention rivers of the Punjab and nearby areas, which is how scholars locate the composition. This is an inference from the text, not an excavation.
- spread to (arrows): the Ganges plain (27.0, 80.0); later South and Southeast Asia (optional, outside this brief).
- scholars split? On the precise dates, yes, and the range is wide. Not on "latter half of the second millennium BCE", the dating used by Jamison and Brereton.

Claims:

1. The Rigveda is the oldest Sanskrit text. It was composed in the second half of the second millennium BCE.
   - [Jamison and Brereton 2020]: the Oxford guide says it is "the oldest Sanskrit text, composed in the latter half of the second millennium bce".
2. It was composed and passed on by voice, not in writing.
   - [Jamison and Brereton 2020]: the "Dating and Authorship" chapter says the hymns were "composed within an oral tradition" and "transmitted only orally for several millennia", so there are no datable manuscripts or inscriptions to fix the date. The hymns were composed by many named poets in family lineages.
   - [UNESCO / PIB 2003] (WEAK, secondary): an Indian government release reports that UNESCO recognised Vedic chanting as heritage of humanity on 7 November 2003, credited to memorisation methods. I found it through a search summary and did not open UNESCO's own record.
3. The language of the Rigveda shows that Indo-Aryan speakers were in the north-west of South Asia by about 1500 BCE, after the decline of the Indus cities.
   - [Narasimhan 2019]: steppe-related ancestry enters South Asia from about 4,000 years ago, and a published preprint of the study links the mid-2nd millennium BCE influx to the early Vedic culture (preprint wording; final paper emphasises spread of Indo-Iranian features).
   - [Shinde 2019]: an Indus person has little steppe ancestry, so steppe-linked ancestry came later.
4. Sanskrit is a cousin, not an ancestor, of Latin, Greek and English.
   - [Haak 2015] and [Narasimhan 2019]: place Sanskrit's ancestor within the same Indo-European spread from the steppe.

Where scholars are closely split: the exact century of composition. [Jamison and Brereton 2020] say "latter half of the second millennium BCE" and say the dating is hard. A minority view in some Indian scholarship holds that Sanskrit developed inside South Asia. Mainstream historical linguistics and ancient DNA do not support that, but the card should not call it settled beyond "second millennium BCE".

Draft card: "The Rigveda is the oldest text in Sanskrit, composed about 3,500 years ago in north-west South Asia. For centuries it was kept alive by memory alone, long before anyone wrote it down."

## Latin and the Romance languages

- id: `latin-romance`
- kind: language
- date range: "Spoken in Latium from about 800 BCE; spread with Roman conquest from the 3rd century BCE; Romance languages take shape in the first millennium CE"
- era year: -100
- location: (41.9, 12.5), radius ~60 km. Rome and Latium.
- location uncertainty: Low for the start (Latium). The spread zone is the whole western empire, so use arrows.
- spread to (arrows): Iberia (40.0, -4.0); Gaul, modern France (46.5, 2.5); Dacia, modern Romania (45.5, 25.0). Optionally North Africa.
- scholars split? Not on the main story. A single scholar (Kramer 2019) argues several Iberian and Romanian varieties were re-spread later in the Middle Ages. Treat that as a detail, not a headline.

Claims:

1. Latin began as the local language of Latium, around Rome, and belonged to a group of closely related languages in Italy. It was "originally spoken in Latium from 800 bce or earlier".
   - [Coleman 2015, OCD]: Latin belongs to the Italic group alongside Faliscan, Umbrian and Oscan, and was originally spoken in Latium from 800 BCE or earlier.
2. As Rome grew, Latin became the common language of Italy first, then of the western Mediterranean and the Balkans.
   - [Coleman 2015, OCD]: "As Roman power grew, Latin became the common language first of Italy, then of the western Mediterranean and Balkan regions".
   - [Mapping History, Univ. of Oregon]: peoples west of the Rhine and south of the Danube "did learn Latin"; the Latin-based languages used "from Portugal to Romania" are the outcome.
3. In some places this took centuries and Latin did not simply wipe out what was there. Julius Caesar began the conquest of Gaul in 58 BCE. The Celtic language Gaulish was probably still spoken alongside Latin two or more centuries after it stopped being written, around the 3rd or 4th century CE.
   - [Blom 2023]: Gaulish "continued to be spoken alongside Latin ... for two, or possibly more, centuries after its written use had petered out, probably in the third or fourth century ce."
   - [Oxford MML blog]: Caesar began his invasion of Gaul in 58 BCE and by the end of the decade Gaul was part of the Roman Empire (a university blog, secondary).
4. The Romance languages (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Romanian and others) are the spoken Latin of ordinary people as it changed in different regions. The first written Romance text is the Strasbourg Oaths of 842 CE.
   - [Coleman 2015, OCD]: the spoken language of the largely illiterate majority, "Vulgar Latin", developed through regional dialects into the Romance languages.
   - [Adams 2007]: Latin was never geographically uniform; the book traces the "beginnings of the Romance dialects in the western Roman provinces".
   - [Ayres-Bennett 1995]: the Strasbourg Oaths (14 February 842) are "the earliest surviving piece of prose in the vernacular of Gaul, and indeed in any Romance language."
5. Dacia (modern Romania) was Roman from 106 CE to about 271 CE.
   - [Kramer 2019]: Dacia "was heavily Latinized from 106 to 271", when Roman military and civil structures were withdrawn (the same article argues that Romanian's Latin comes largely from varieties south of the Danube; that is one scholar's argument, so I would not repeat it on the card).

Where scholars are closely split: not on the broad picture. Details such as how much of modern Spanish, Portuguese and Romanian continues Latin spoken locally in Roman times, and how quickly local languages disappeared, are debated by specialists.

Draft card: "Latin started as the speech of Rome and its neighbours. As Roman armies and towns spread, so did Latin, and over the following centuries it split into Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian and more."

WEAK / dropped: exact conquest dates other than Caesar's 58 BCE (only a university blog) and Dacia 106 CE; the Praeneste fibula, Duenos vase and Lapis Niger dates (search summaries from mirrored encyclopedia text; the fibula's authenticity is also contested). Kramer's claim about the Reconquista was left out of the card text.

## Germanic, and English as a descendant

- id: `germanic-english`
- kind: branch
- date range: "A distinct Germanic branch by about 500 BCE in southern Scandinavia and northern Germany; English reaches Britain from about the 5th century CE"
- era year: 450
- location: (56.0, 10.0), radius ~400 km. Southern Scandinavia and the north German coast.
- location uncertainty: Medium. Linguists and archaeologists agree on the region; the precise sub-area and how the branch got there from the steppe is still being worked out.
- spread to (arrows): England (52.5, 0.0); the Rhine and Low Countries (51.5, 6.0); central and southern Europe (48.0, 14.0). Iceland and North America are outside the scope.
- scholars split? On how Germanic speech reached Scandinavia (steppe-descended Corded Ware people around 2800 BCE, or a later Bronze Age movement, per the 2024 preprint). Not on English being a Germanic language.

Claims:

1. Most archaeologists and linguists agree that southern Scandinavia and northern Germany were home to speakers of an Indo-European language from about 3000 BCE, and that a distinct Germanic branch had formed by about 500 BCE.
   - [Faarlund 2008]: "most archaeologists and historical linguists seem to have reached the consensus" about the third-millennium BCE settlement and a "distinct branch of Indo-European ... by c. 500 BC".
2. English is a Germanic language. Its history can be traced from Proto-Germanic through Proto-West-Germanic to Old English around 900 CE.
   - [Ringe and Taylor 2014]: the volume "traces the development of English phonology and morphology from Proto-Germanic to Old English of about the year 900".
3. English came to Britain with migrants from across the North Sea after Roman rule ended. Eastern England got up to 76% of its ancestry from the continental North Sea region in early medieval times.
   - [Gretzinger 2022]: DNA from 460 medieval northwestern Europeans shows "large-scale substantial migration across the North Sea into Britain during the Early Middle Ages", with up to 76% of ancestry in eastern England.
4. Steppe-descended ancestry reached Scandinavia in the Neolithic, and a 2024 preprint proposes a further Bronze Age movement within Scandinavia linked to the later spread of Germanic speech.
   - [McColl 2024] (preprint, not peer reviewed): a movement "originating in the east", 800 years after the Corded Ware culture, which the authors link to the later expansion of Germanic. The authors say the interpretations are complex and potentially controversial. Mark as WEAK until published.

Where scholars are closely split: how and when Proto-Germanic formed in Scandinavia, and whether the Anglo-Saxon movement was a few elites or many families. Gretzinger et al. argue for many; earlier studies argued for fewer.

Draft card: "English belongs to the Germanic branch, which took shape in southern Scandinavia and northern Germany. Settlers crossing the North Sea after Roman rule ended brought the ancestor of English to Britain."

## Uralic

- id: `uralic`
- kind: family
- date range: "Common ancestor about 4,500 to 6,000 years ago; spread west across northern Eurasia about 4,200 to 3,900 years ago"
- era year: -2200
- location: (58.0, 60.0), radius ~900 km, centred on the Urals region.
- location uncertainty: High. Two serious proposals sit on opposite sides of the mountains: west Siberia east of the Urals, or the Volga-Kama region west of them. Draw a soft region over the Urals. Alternative points: west Siberia (58.0, 68.0); Kama valley (57.0, 53.0).
- spread to (arrows): Volga region (55.0, 46.0); Finland and the Baltic (61.0, 26.0); Sápmi, northern Scandinavia (68.0, 22.0); the Carpathian Basin by the Hungarians (47.0, 19.5), shown in the Hungarian stop.
- scholars split? Yes, on the homeland and the age.

Claims:

1. The Uralic family includes Finnish, Estonian, Sami, Hungarian and more, though these languages are spread far apart.
   - [Honkola 2013]: uses the "Uralic language family" with Finnic, Saami and Finno-Ugric branches, and finds divergences related to climate and population movement.
   - [Lamnidis 2018]: describes Finns and Saami as populations "speaking Uralic languages".
2. One recent study puts the homeland east of the Urals, in western Siberia, and the westward spread at 4,200 to 3,900 years ago, driven by a cold dry climate shift, a bronze-trade network and contact with Indo-Iranian speakers.
   - [Grünthal 2022]: "We argue that the Uralic homeland was east of the Urals and initially out of contact with Indo-European."
   - [Univ. of Helsinki 2022]: the spread "began in western Siberia during the early Metal Age", westward expansion roughly 4,200 to 3,900 years ago, and the family is about 4,500 years old.
3. Another view puts the homeland at the Kama river west of the Urals, with the spread tied to the Seima-Turbino bronze network of about 2200 to 1900 BCE.
   - [Parpola 2022]: argues for "the Kama River Valley as the Uralic homeland", and that early Uralic words were borrowed from pre-Indo-Iranian speakers.
4. Siberian ancestry arrived in north-eastern Europe at least 3,500 years ago and is strongest in today's Uralic speakers. Ancestors of the Saami once lived over a larger area than today.
   - [Lamnidis 2018]: "migrations from Siberia that began at least 3500 years ago", admixed "particularly into populations speaking Uralic languages today", and "ancestors of modern Saami inhabited a larger territory during the Iron Age".
5. Estimates of the age range from about 4,500 years (Helsinki release for Grünthal 2022) to about 6,000. A 6,000-year figure appears in a search summary of a conference talk by Janhunen, and I did not open it, so it is WEAK. [Honkola 2013] found later divergence dates for Finno-Ugric, Finnic and Saami than earlier linguistic studies.

Where scholars are closely split: homeland (west Siberia vs the Kama valley, with a further option of further east in Siberia), and age. Linguists agree Uralic is a real family and that its branches have been in contact with Indo-Iranian and later Germanic and Slavic speakers.

Draft card: "Finnish, Estonian, Sami and Hungarian are related, though they are now far apart. Their ancestor was spoken somewhere around the Urals about 4,500 years ago or more, and it spread west across the forests of northern Eurasia."

## Hungarian

- id: `hungarian`
- kind: language
- date range: "Moves west to the Carpathian Basin about 895 to 905 CE"
- era year: 895
- location: (47.0, 19.5), radius ~250 km. The Carpathian Basin (Great Hungarian Plain). The tribes had been in the Etelköz area on the Pontic steppe (about 47.0, 32.0) before.
- location uncertainty: Low for the Carpathian Basin as the destination. The earlier homeland of the Magyars is much less certain.
- spread to (arrows): from the Pontic steppe at (47.0, 32.0) to the Carpathian Basin (47.0, 19.5). An optional faint arrow from the Urals (see uralic).
- scholars split? Mostly no on language history. Hungarian is Uralic and arrived with the Magyars. A genetics paper argues the language might have been present earlier in the basin; that is a minority view.

Claims:

1. Hungarian is a Uralic language. Its closest relatives are Khanty and Mansi in western Siberia, though some scholars treat the "Ugric" grouping as a contact area rather than a family branch.
   - [Neparáczki 2018]: describes the "Finno-Ugric Hungarian language, originated from proto Uralic people" as the widely accepted view.
   - [Oxford Guide to the Uralic Languages] (WEAK, from a search summary): Ugric as Hungarian plus Khanty and Mansi, with many scholars now arguing the shared Ugric features may reflect contact rather than inheritance. I did not open the page.
2. The Magyars (Hungarians), a confederation of seven tribes under Árpád, moved from the Pontic steppe and occupied the Carpathian Basin about 895 to 905 CE.
   - [Neparáczki 2018]: the Conquerors "arrived from the Pontic steppe and occupied the Carpathian Basin at 895-905 AD as a confederation of seven tribes led by Árpád".
   - [Neparáczki 2019]: "conquering Hungarians seem to be a recently assembled population" with European, Asian and mixed components from Central-Inner Asian and Pontic steppe sources.
3. The arriving group was not a pure "Finno-Ugric" population. They carried Central Asian and steppe ancestry, and they did not leave a large share of today's Hungarians' ancestry, so language and ancestry do not line up neatly.
   - [Neparáczki 2018]: "the Conquerors did not have a major contribution to the gene pool of the Carpathian Basin." The authors suggest this raises doubts about the Conquerors as the source of the language, and that the language might have been in the basin before. This is the authors' view and is a minority position among linguists.
4. Date caution: traditional Hungarian history gives 895 or 896 for the conquest. The settlement lasted years.
   - [Neparáczki 2018]: gives 895-905.

Where scholars are closely split: mostly not split on the main story (Hungarian arrived in 895-905), but the genetic argument above is a challenge to the conventional version; I would not put it on the card.

## Turkic

- id: `turkic`
- kind: family
- date range: "Common ancestor about 2,100 years ago; Göktürk empire from the 540s CE; Old Turkic inscriptions in the 8th century CE"
- era year: 550
- location: (47.5, 102.5), radius ~600 km. Mongolia, the Orkhon valley, where the Göktürk khaganate was based.
- location uncertainty: Medium-high. The ancestor language's homeland is debated (Mongolia and southern Siberia is the conventional view; one 2021 study traces the wider "Transeurasian" ancestry to Northeast Asian farmers). The Göktürk centre in Mongolia is secure.
- spread to (arrows): Kazakh steppe (48.0, 68.0); the Pontic-Caspian steppe and Volga (48.0, 45.0); Central Asia (40.0, 65.0); Anatolia (39.0, 35.0) in the next stop.
- scholars split? Yes on the earliest homeland and on whether Turkic belongs to a larger "Transeurasian" family (also Mongolic, Tungusic, Korean, Japanese). Not on the 6th-century westward spread.

Claims:

1. Turkic languages (Turkish, Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Uzbek, Uyghur and others) come from one ancestor spoken about 2,100 years ago.
   - [Savelyev and Robbeets 2020]: a Bayesian tree of 32 Turkic varieties infers the time-depth of the family "at around 2100 years before present".
   - [Findley 2005]: "Beginning in Inner Asia two thousand years ago, the Turks have migrated and expanded" across Eurasia.
2. The Türks (Göktürks) emerged in Mongolia in the 540s CE and shifted the balance of power there. They are the first people with a surviving Turkic-language text.
   - [Sinor 1990]: "a little-known people appeared on the Chinese horizon in the 540s" and "quickly shifted the balance of power in Mongolia"; the Türks are "the first people with a surviving Turkic-language text".
3. The Orkhon inscriptions (Kül Tigin and Bilge Khagan monuments) in Mongolia, from the 8th century CE, are the oldest long Turkic texts. They are written in a runic-looking Old Turkic alphabet.
   - [Sinor 1990]: the Türks left the first surviving Turkic-language text.
   - WEAK for the exact years 732 and 735: only search summaries of encyclopedia text, and the Britannica page would not load. Say "8th century" on the card.
4. Over the first millennium CE the steppe shifted from Indo-European speakers (such as Scythian) to Turkic speakers, as eastern nomads moved west.
   - [Damgaard 2018 Nature]: 137 ancient genomes show the steppes changed "from being inhabited by Indo-European speakers of largely West Eurasian ancestry to the mostly Turkic-speaking groups of the present day, who are primarily of East Asian ancestry", with eastern nomads moving west from about the 2nd to 3rd century BCE and into Medieval khanates.

Where scholars are closely split: the earliest homeland and whether Turkic is linked to Mongolic, Tungusic, Korean and Japanese. [Robbeets 2021] argues the "Transeurasian" family spread with early farmers in Northeast Asia, challenging the older view of steppe pastoralists. Many historical linguists still dispute that these languages form one family.

Draft card: "Turkic languages, from Turkish to Kazakh and Uyghur, began in the steppe lands of Mongolia and southern Siberia. From about the 6th century CE, Turkic-speaking peoples spread west across thousands of kilometres of steppe."

## Turkish in Anatolia

- id: `turkish-anatolia`
- kind: language
- date range: "From 1071 CE onward"
- era year: 1071
- location: (39.0, 35.0), radius ~500 km. Anatolia (modern Turkey). Manzikert (Malazgirt), where Byzantium lost in 1071, is at about (39.15, 42.54).
- location uncertainty: Low for Anatolia. The 1071 battle site is approximate.
- spread to (arrows): from Central Asia (40.0, 65.0), through Iran (36.0, 52.0), to Anatolia (39.0, 35.0).
- scholars split? No on 1071 as the turning point, but see below: it was not a sudden conquest.

Claims:

1. The Seljuk Turks defeated the Byzantine emperor at the Battle of Manzikert (Malazgirt) in August 1071. After that, Byzantium could not stop Turkish settlers entering Anatolia.
   - [Fleet 2009]: the Byzantine emperor Romanos Diogenes "was defeated by the Turkomans at the battle of Malazgirt (Manzikert) in August 1071", after which the Byzantines could not halt "the Turkish flow into Anatolia".
2. It was not a conquest of Anatolia in one go. The Seljuk ruler Alp Arslan was aiming at Syria and Egypt, and Turks had raided and appeared in Anatolia earlier. A slow Turkification followed.
   - [Fleet 2009]: Alp Arslan "aimed to move against Syria and Egypt rather than take Anatolia"; Turkoman raids had weakened Byzantine Anatolia for many years; "a slow Turkification began".
3. Anatolia had spoken Greek, Armenian, Kurdish and other languages for centuries. Before 1071, Anatolian languages such as Hittite and Luwian had long disappeared.
   - [Damgaard 2018 Science]: Indo-European languages (Anatolian branch) are attested in Bronze Age Anatolia. This supports "Anatolia was Indo-European-speaking before Turkic".
   - Inference rather than a direct quote; I did not find a source that lists the languages of Anatolia in 1071, so drop this claim if it needs a citation on the card.

Where scholars are closely split: how much of the later Turkish-speaking population came from Central Asian migration and how much from local people who switched to Turkish. I did not find a source I could open on this, so the card should say "slowly" and stop there.

Draft card: "In 1071 a Turkish-led army beat the Byzantine emperor at Manzikert. Turkish speakers moved into Anatolia over the following centuries, and Turkish slowly became the main language of what is now Turkey."

## Arabic

- id: `arabic`
- kind: language
- date range: "Written from the 4th century CE; spreads out of Arabia with the Islamic conquests from the 630s CE"
- era year: 650
- location: (24.5, 39.6), radius ~600 km. The Hejaz and western Arabia (Medina at about 24.47, 39.61; Mecca at about 21.4, 39.8).
- location uncertainty: Medium. Arabic was spoken across the peninsula and its northern edges before Islam; the Hejaz is the best-known centre of the early Islamic state.
- spread to (arrows): Syria and Iraq (33.0, 38.0); Egypt (30.0, 31.0); North Africa/Maghreb (34.0, 5.0); Iberia (37.5, -4.5); Persia and Central Asia (36.0, 60.0).
- scholars split? On how fast the shift happened, yes. Not on the conquests starting the spread. Arabic belongs to the Semitic branch of the Afroasiatic family (another stop covers Afroasiatic).

Claims:

1. Arabic was already written in the 4th century CE. The oldest well-known long Arabic text is a tomb inscription at Namara, in southern Syria, in the Nabataean script, dated 328 or 332 CE. It names Imru' al-Qays as "king of all the Arabs".
   - [Louvre catalogue]: lintel from En Nemara, language Arabic, script Nabataean, funerary epitaph of Imru' al-Qays, "king of all the Arabs"; the inscription gives the year 332 CE and the catalogue also lists 328 CE.
2. Arabic spread out of Arabia with the armies and settlers of the early Islamic conquests, from the 630s CE. Arabic then became the main language of the Middle East and North Africa.
   - [Pat-El and Stokes 2022]: Aramaic had been "the dominant common language in the Levant for about a thousand years before the Muslim conquests", and Arabic replaced it there after the conquests.
   - [Owens 2013]: the Arabic classical tradition is "attested beginning 1400 years ago"; today Arabic is spoken natively by about 300 million people.
3. The change from Aramaic, Coptic and Berber to Arabic was gradual and uneven, and scholars disagree on how it happened. It was not a sudden switch after the conquests.
   - [Pat-El and Stokes 2022]: the spread of Arabic and the details of language shift "are still poorly understood"; many assume a rapid post-conquest shift, but the authors argue the evidence fits "a long period of contact between bilingual communities" better.

Where scholars are closely split: how quickly local populations switched to Arabic, and whether features of today's dialects show Aramaic influence from rapid, imperfect learning or from long bilingual contact. The card should say "over centuries".

Draft card: "Arabic was spoken across the Arabian Peninsula long before Islam. With the Islamic conquests in the 7th century CE it spread west to Spain and east to Central Asia, and over the next centuries became the everyday language of most of the Middle East and North Africa."

WEAK / dropped: Arabic as the state language of Egypt in 706 and of the administration under Abd al-Malik. These came from search summaries of Britannica pages that I could not open, so they are not in the claims above. The "Aramaic, Coptic and Berber replaced by Arabic in the 11th century" timings also rest on Britannica and Wiley summaries I could not open.

## Basque

- id: `basque`
- kind: language
- date range: "Present in western Europe before and through the Roman period; older than the arrival of Indo-European languages there"
- era year: 100
- location: (43.0, -2.0), radius ~120 km. The Basque Country on both sides of the western Pyrenees.
- location uncertainty: Low for where Basque is spoken. The area was larger in the past and the language's age and origin are uncertain.
- spread to (arrows): none. It is a language that stayed put while Indo-European languages spread around it.
- scholars split? On origin and age, yes. Not on Basque being unrelated to Latin, Spanish or French.

Claims:

1. Basque is not an Indo-European language and has no accepted relatives. It is described as "the sole survivor of the very ancient languages of Western Europe", and the long search for its relatives has not produced an agreed answer.
   - [Trask 1997, publisher description]: "sole survivor of the very ancient languages of Western Europe" and "a long critical evaluation of the search for its relatives".
2. Indo-European speakers reached Iberia by about 2000 BCE, yet Basque survived beside them. Today's Basques are genetically best described as a typical Iron Age Iberian population, without the later mixing that affected the rest of Iberia.
   - [Olalde 2019]: by about 2000 BCE people with steppe ancestry replaced 40% of Iberian ancestry and nearly 100% of Y-chromosomes; steppe ancestry spread "into non-Indo-European-speaking" regions too; present-day Basques are "best described as a typical Iron Age population without the admixture events that later affected the rest of Iberia."
3. Basque's ancestor language (Aquitanian) is known from Roman-era inscriptions in Latin letters, mostly names of people and gods.
   - [Gorrochategui 2022]: the chapter's title, "The Relationship between Aquitanian and Basque", confirms the topic; I could not read the text.
   - WEAK: that the inscriptions date to the 1st-2nd centuries CE and that Aquitanian is the ancestor of Basque rests on search summaries of encyclopedia-style pages.

Where scholars are closely split: where Basque came from, and whether it is the last survivor of a wider pre-Indo-European Europe. Proposed links to other families (such as the Caucasian languages) are not accepted by most linguists. I did not verify that last sentence in a fetched source, so keep the card claim general.

Draft card: "Basque is the odd one out in western Europe: it is not related to Spanish, French or any other Indo-European language. It is thought to be the last survivor of the languages spoken there before Indo-European languages arrived."
