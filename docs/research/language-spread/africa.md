## Modern humans in Africa

- id: `africa-modern-humans`
- kind: migration (no better fit; it is an origin stop with no arrows of its own)
- date range: "~300,000 years ago and later"
- era year: -300000
- location: (4.0, 22.0), radius ~3500 km, i.e. the whole continent. Reference fossil point: Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (31.85, -8.87).
- location uncertainty: High on purpose. The earliest fossils come from Morocco, Ethiopia and South Africa, and the genetic and fossil evidence points to a population spread across Africa, not one birthplace. Do not pin this to a single dot.
- spread to: none (the next stop carries the arrows)
- scholars split? Mildly. The classic "one cradle in East Africa" picture is being replaced by a "many connected populations across Africa" picture. The 300,000-year age for the earliest Homo sapiens features is the commonly used figure, but "modern" skull shape appears later, so where you draw the line for "our species" is a judgement call.

Claims:

1. Our species, Homo sapiens, evolved in Africa about 300,000 years ago.
   - [Smithsonian HOP]: "During a time of dramatic climate change 300,000 years ago, Homo sapiens evolved in Africa."
   - [Hublin 2017]: fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, dated by thermoluminescence to 315 ± 34 thousand years, show a mix of modern-looking face, jaw and teeth with a more primitive braincase, and are the oldest site documenting early stages of the H. sapiens clade.
2. Our species did not arise in one small corner of Africa. The processes that produced modern humans involved the whole continent, with populations that were partly separated and partly connected.
   - [Hublin 2017]: "the evolutionary processes behind the emergence of H. sapiens involved the whole African continent."
   - [Scerri 2018]: fossil, archaeological and genetic evidence "support an emerging view of a highly structured African prehistory" with morphologically varied H. sapiens populations throughout Africa.
3. Genetic data show the deepest splits between living human groups are very old, and they are inside Africa.
   - [Schlebusch 2017]: the first modern human population divergence is estimated at 350,000 to 260,000 years ago.
   - [Mallick 2016]: the ancestors of some pairs of living populations were substantially separated by 100,000 years ago.

Draft card text: "Our species, Homo sapiens, appeared in Africa roughly 300,000 years ago. The oldest fossils turn up from Morocco to Ethiopia to South Africa, which suggests many connected groups across the continent, not one single homeland."

## Where and when language began: nobody knows

- id: `language-origin-unknown`
- kind: none of the four fits. Suggest a note card with no arrows. If the schema forces a value, use migration.
- date range: "unknown; most likely well before ~70,000 years ago"
- era year: null (any year here would be false precision)
- location: none. If the layout needs a dot, reuse the Africa marker (4.0, 22.0) with radius ~3500 km and label it "no known location".
- location uncertainty: Total. There is no evidence that places the beginning of language anywhere.
- spread to: none
- scholars split? Yes, strongly, on when. Some argue language is very old and shared with other human species. Others say there is simply no usable evidence. Both agree speech leaves no fossil.
- scope note: do not reconstruct a first language. Mainstream historical linguists reject claims of a single reconstructed ancestor of all languages ("Proto-World").

Claims:

1. Speech does not fossilise, and the physical and archaeological evidence cannot tell us when or how language began.
   - [Hauser 2014]: "the fossil and archaeological evidence does not inform our understanding of the computations and representations of our earliest ancestors, leaving details of origins and selective pressure unresolved"; the authors conclude the "most fundamental questions about the origins and evolution of our linguistic capacity remain as mysterious as ever."
2. A gene linked to speech, FOXP2, is a clue, not proof, and its story keeps changing.
   - [Krause 2007]: Neandertals carry the same two human-specific FOXP2 changes, so those changes predate the split of modern humans and Neandertals (about 300,000-400,000 years ago).
   - [Atkinson 2018]: a reanalysis of hundreds of genomes found "no evidence of recent positive or balancing selection at FOXP2," revising the idea that it marks a recent leap.
3. Every living human group has full language, and the capacity is shared by all humans, so it very probably existed before our ancestors spread around the world. This is an inference, not a measured date.
   - [Hauser 2014]: language competence is "a biological capacity shared by all humans" (stated as the view of the authors and many language scientists).
   - [Mallick 2016] and [Schlebusch 2017] show living groups whose ancestors separated 100,000 years ago or more. WEAK as a single logical step: the papers establish the genetic separation dates; the language inference is ours and is the standard reasoning, not a finding of those papers.
4. Estimates of how old language is range from tens of thousands of years to around half a million.
   - [Dediu 2013]: argue recognisably modern language is likely an ancient feature of our genus, predating the common ancestor of modern humans and Neandertals "about half a million years ago," against the "usually quoted 50,000-100,000 years."
   - [Hauser 2014] hold that the evidence cannot settle it. Treat these two as opposite ends of an open debate.
5. Most historical linguists reject reconstructing one ancestor of all languages.
   - [Campbell 2008], as summarised in [Erschler 2009]: the chapter on Proto-World concludes that "extremely little that is definite can be said or hypothesized about such a language." The same reviewer calls the book polemical, so this is one strong, not neutral, statement.
   - [Campbell 2008] (Crossref/publisher description) lists Proto-World among "prominent and more controversial proposals of distant genetic relationship" that the book evaluates.
   - Note: the stronger line that the proposals "have been rejected by the majority of practicing historical linguists" is the book's own introduction, which I only saw relayed through a search summary. WEAK for that exact wording; use the Erschler quote.

Draft card text: "Nobody knows where or when language began. Speech leaves no fossils, and the genes and bones we can study do not settle it. What we do know is that every human group alive today has a full language, so the ability is very probably older than the journeys out of Africa."

## Leaving Africa

- id: `out-of-africa-dispersal`
- kind: migration
- date range: "~70,000-50,000 years ago (earlier, smaller trips probably happened)"
- era year: -60000
- location: Horn of Africa / Bab-el-Mandeb strait (12.6, 43.3), radius ~600 km. Alternative gateway: Sinai (29.5, 33.5).
- location uncertainty: The exit route is open. Genetic work favours Egypt/Sinai in one study; archaeology and sea-level work keep the Bab-el-Mandeb crossing alive. The dot is a symbol for "Northeast Africa exit", not a confirmed crossing point.
- spread to (approximate): Arabia / Persian Gulf (25.0, 55.0); South Asia (21.0, 78.0); Sahul / Australia (-12.5, 132.9). A fourth arrow into Europe or East Asia belongs to the Eurasia stops.
- scholars split? Yes, on route, on timing, and on whether there was one wave or several (details below).

Claims:

1. The ancestors of almost all living non-Africans left Africa in a main dispersal about 50,000-70,000 years ago.
   - [Malaspinas 2016]: Aboriginal Australians and Papuans diverged from Eurasians 51-72 thousand years ago "following a single out-of-Africa dispersal."
   - [Pagani 2015]: genetic split times from non-Africans estimated at about 55,000 years (Egyptians), 65,000 (Ethiopians) and 75,000 (West Africans).
   - [Bae 2017] describe the traditional model as "a single wave at ~60,000 years ago."
2. Earlier, smaller trips out of Africa almost certainly happened, and they did not all vanish.
   - [Hershkovitz 2018]: a jaw from Misliya Cave, Israel, dated to 177,000-194,000 years ago, shows Homo sapiens left Africa earlier than thought; the older Levant sites Skhul and Qafzeh date to 90,000-120,000 years ago.
   - [Armitage 2011]: stone tools at Jebel Faya, United Arab Emirates, show humans in eastern Arabia during the last interglacial (the paper's own dating, well before 60,000 years ago).
   - [Pagani 2016]: about 2% of Papuan genomes may come from "an early and largely extinct expansion" out of Africa earlier than 75,000 years ago.
   - [Bae 2017]: "growing evidence for multiple dispersals predating 60,000 years ago," which left "at least some genetic traces."
3. The route out is still debated: across the Bab-el-Mandeb strait into Arabia, or via Egypt and Sinai.
   - [Pagani 2015]: "Two alternative routes, via Egypt and Sinai or across the Bab el Mandeb strait into Arabia, have traditionally been proposed"; their genomes of Egyptians and Ethiopians point to Egypt as "the more likely gateway" (a northern route).
   - [Armitage 2011] argue for a southern route into Arabia ("The southern route out of Africa").
   - Both are real, peer-reviewed positions; the card should say "by land or across a narrow sea" without picking one.

Disagreement to state neutrally: some researchers see one main exit about 60,000 years ago with small earlier failures; others see several dispersals, some leaving DNA in living people.

Draft card text: "Around 70,000 to 50,000 years ago, a group of modern humans left Africa, probably by the Horn of Africa or the Sinai, and their descendants spread across the rest of the world. Smaller, earlier trips almost certainly happened too."

## Reaching Australia and New Guinea (Sahul)

- id: `sahul-arrival`
- kind: migration
- date range: "~65,000-50,000 years ago"
- era year: -60000
- location: Madjedbebe rock shelter, Arnhem Land (-12.5, 132.9) as a reference site; the whole ancient landmass of Sahul (Australia plus New Guinea) is the destination.
- location uncertainty: Moderate for the site (excavated, coordinates published), high for "where people first landed": the coast then was far from today's, and earlier sites may be underwater.
- spread to: none (arrival stop). The origin is the previous stop.
- scholars split? Yes, on the date.

Claims:

1. Humans were in northern Australia by roughly 65,000 years ago, according to the excavation team.
   - [Clarkson 2017]: occupation at Madjedbebe "began around 65,000 years ago" (conservatively 59,300 years, after subtracting the dating uncertainty), with ground-edge hatchets and seed-grinding. The authors say this "sets a new minimum age for the human colonization of Australia and the dispersal of modern humans out of Africa and across south Asia."
2. This date is disputed. Other researchers say the best case is about 50,000 years.
   - [O'Connell 2018]: "an age estimate of >50 ka for this site is unlikely to be valid," while allowing humans "may have moved far beyond Africa well before 50-55 ka."
   - [Clarkson 2017] note earlier estimates for first colonisation of Australia ranged from 47,000 to about 60,000 years ago.
3. DNA from living Aboriginal Australians points to a single founding population and a separation from Eurasians 51,000-72,000 years ago.
   - [Malaspinas 2016]: "all of the studied Aboriginal Australians descend from a single founding population that differentiated ~10-32 kya"; Aboriginal Australians and Papuans diverged from Eurasians 51-72 kya.

Draft card text: "Within a few thousand years of leaving Africa, people reached Australia and New Guinea, which were joined in one landmass then. The oldest known site in northern Australia has been dated to about 65,000 years ago, though some researchers think 50,000 is safer."

## Afroasiatic (family)

- id: `afroasiatic`
- kind: family
- date range: "roughly 10,000-15,000 years ago for the common ancestor, with no agreed figure"
- era year: -10000
- location: (18.0, 31.0), radius ~1200 km: the Nile valley between Upper Egypt and the Sudan, with the eastern Sahara and Horn to the south and east. Alternative minority placement: the Levant (32.0, 35.5).
- location uncertainty: High. The homeland is genuinely debated (see split note). The Nile marker is a compromise inside the majority view, not a finding.
- spread to (approximate, branch regions from Glottolog family centres): Egypt and the Nile Delta (28.9, 31.0); the Maghreb and Sahara, Berber (25.9, 1.5); Lake Chad, Chadic (10.4, 14.0); the Horn, Cushitic (5.3, 37.8). Semitic (29.2, 41.8) is the next stop.
- scholars split? Yes. Where it began: northeast Africa (most scholars) versus the Levant (a minority). How old: a range, because the methods that give dates are contested. Which branches belong: Omotic.

Claims:

1. Afroasiatic is a large family of related languages, with branches including Semitic (Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic), Egyptian (ancient), Berber, Chadic (Hausa), Cushitic (Somali, Oromo) and Omotic.
   - [Huehnergard 2008]: the family "comprises at least five and as many as eight branches."
   - [Glottolog]: lists Berber, Chadic, Cushitic, Egyptian and Semitic as branches; about 380 languages in current data (v5.2.1 data, counted from the CLDF export).
   - Branch count differs because of Omotic, below.
2. Whether Omotic is part of the family is disputed, which is why the number of branches varies.
   - [Glottolog]: Omotic "does not show diagnostic Afro-Asiatic features" and "is therefore excluded until such features can be shown" (citing Hayward, Bender, Theil and others).
   - [Huehnergard 2008]: "at least five and as many as eight" branches reflects the same uncertainty. Several earlier specialists (for example Bender, cited in [Ehret 2004]) treat Omotic as a full branch.
3. Scholars do not agree where Afroasiatic began. Most place it in northeast Africa; a minority place it in the Levant.
   - [Huehnergard 2008]: most scholars place the homeland "somewhere in the vicinity of the center of the family's current geographical range," some further east in southern Egypt or northern Sudan, and "a few scholars" argue for southwest Asia.
   - [Ehret 2004] (submitted version): argue for "the southeastern Sahara or adjacent Horn of Africa," and for a pre-farming proto-language, against a Near Eastern farming origin proposed by Diamond and Bellwood.
   - [Militarev 2002]: argues the speakers were "with high probability" the Natufian and Post-Natufian cultures of the Levant. [Blazek] (conference text) follows a Levantine scenario.
4. Age: there is no agreed date, but even the youngest proposals make it an old family, around the start of farming or earlier.
   - [Militarev 2002]: estimates the break-up of the main branches in "the ninth millennium BC" (about 11,000 years ago) as "the most realistic and cautious dating."
   - [Blazek]: recalibrated word-list counting gives about -10,000 for the first split (G. Starostin 2010: -10,010; Militarev 2005: -9,970). Authorship from the PDF metadata; the method is contested and the paper itself says classical glottochronology "was discredited."
   - Ehret's older, larger figure (at least 15,000 years) reached me only through a search summary: WEAK, not used.

Draft card text: "Afroasiatic is a family of related languages that includes Arabic, Hebrew, ancient Egyptian, Berber, Hausa, Somali and Amharic. It is probably more than 10,000 years old. Scholars still argue about whether it began in northeast Africa or in the Levant."

## Semitic reaches the Horn of Africa

- id: `semitic-into-the-horn`
- kind: branch
- date range: "~2,800 years ago (estimate for the Ethiopian Semitic branch)"
- era year: -800
- location: from southern Arabia (15.0, 45.0) to the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands (11.0, 39.5)
- location uncertainty: Moderate. The study names southern Arabia as the source and the Horn as the target; the exact crossing and the first settlements are not located.
- spread to: highlands of Ethiopia and Eritrea (11.0, 39.5)
- scholars split? Yes, partly. A Bayesian word-list study supports an Arabian crossing; earlier scholarship (for example Diakonoff, as discussed in [Ehret 2004]) put Semitic's roots inside Africa, near the Nile Delta and Sinai.

Claims:

1. Semitic as a whole is estimated to have begun in the Levant about 5,750 years ago, not in Africa.
   - [Kitchen 2009]: "We estimate an Early Bronze Age origin for Semitic approximately 5750 years ago in the Levant"; its tests favour Akkadian splitting off first "over competing hypotheses (e.g. an African origin of Semitic)."
2. The Ethiopian Semitic languages (Amharic, Tigrinya and relatives) are estimated to descend from a single arrival from southern Arabia about 2,800 years ago.
   - [Kitchen 2009]: "contemporary Ethiosemitic languages of Africa reflect a single introduction of early Ethiosemitic from southern Arabia approximately 2800 years ago."
   - Single source (a peer-reviewed phylogenetic dating study using lexical data). ONE-SOURCE: keep, but word as "estimated".
3. Its Afroasiatic neighbours in the Horn (Cushitic, Omotic) are older residents of the region. This is a general map fact and sits inside claim 3 of the Afroasiatic stop.
   - [Huehnergard 2008] and [Glottolog] for the branch list.

Draft card text: "Semitic languages are one branch of Afroasiatic. One dating study puts Semitic's start in the Levant about 5,750 years ago, and estimates that the Ethiopian Semitic languages came across the Red Sea from southern Arabia about 2,800 years ago."

## Niger-Congo / Atlantic-Congo (family)

- id: `niger-congo-atlantic-congo`
- kind: family
- date range: "several thousand years old; no agreed age" (the best-dated part is Bantu, ~5,000 years)
- era year: null. If one is required, tie it to the Bantu root: -3000.
- location: (9.0, -2.0), radius ~2500 km: West Africa and the Sahel belt. Do not put a precise dot; no source gives a homeland for the whole family.
- location uncertainty: Very high for the family as a whole. Only the Bantu branch has a located homeland (next stop).
- spread to: the next stop shows the one expansion that is well documented (Bantu). Optional arrow: West Africa to central Africa (4.0, 12.0).
- scholars split? Yes, on where the family's boundary lies. "Niger-Congo" (Greenberg's wide family) is used by many Africanists; Glottolog, a major classification, accepts only a narrower Atlantic-Congo core.

Claims:

1. This is the biggest family of languages in the world by head-count of languages, with about 1,400 languages in Glottolog's Atlantic-Congo family, including around 560 Bantu languages.
   - [Glottolog]: counted from the 5.2.1 CLDF export: Atlantic-Congo 1,408 languages (Austronesian 1,276; Indo-European 586). "Narrow Bantu" subtree 563 languages in the 5.3 tree. These are counts of Glottolog entries and include some extinct languages; use "about 1,400" and "more than 500".
   - [Fortes-Lima 2024]: "more than 500 Bantu languages" spoken by about 350 million people (this one is a count of Bantu only, from the paper's introduction).
2. Whether all of "Niger-Congo" is one family is debated. Glottolog keeps a core and sets aside several groups for now.
   - [Glottolog]: the Atlantic-Congo core "is held together by regular sound correspondences" plus noun class systems and verb extensions; Mande, Kordofanian, Ijoid, Kru and Dogon "have not yet been shown to stand up to these criteria wherefore they are excluded" (Mande's own entry says it "has not yet been shown to contain systematic sound correspondences, noun class systems or verbal extensions with Niger-Congo in some form").
   - [Guldemann 2018]: argues the publicly available evidence supports accepting Niger-Congo only in a narrower sense (relayed through the author's colloquium abstract, which says he reassesses Greenberg's four-family map of Africa; the "narrower sense" wording is from a lecture summary I only saw through search: WEAK on the exact wording, solid on the general point that the reassessment exists).
   - [Campbell 2008], as quoted by [Guldemann 2026 abstract]: Greenberg's continental classification is "badly in need of major reinvestigation and reworking."
3. Africanists have been called "lumpers" by comparative linguists elsewhere, which is part of why the boundaries are argued over.
   - [Dimmendaal 2008] (abstract): "Africanists have been criticized by comparative linguists working on language families in other parts of the world for being lumpers."

Draft card text: "Atlantic-Congo, often called Niger-Congo, is the largest language family on Earth by number of languages, with about 1,400. It includes the Bantu languages. Linguists still disagree about how many West African groups really belong to it."

## The Bantu expansion

- id: `bantu-expansion`
- kind: migration
- date range: "~5,000-1,500 years ago (starting 4,000-5,000 years ago and continuing for millennia)"
- era year: -3000
- location: the border region of Nigeria and Cameroon, around the Cameroon highlands (6.0, 10.5), radius ~300 km
- location uncertainty: Moderate. Linguists, archaeologists and geneticists agree on the region; the exact starting place inside it is not pinned down.
- spread to (approximate): the Congo basin (-3.0, 20.0); the Great Lakes (-1.5, 31.0); the East African coast (-6.0, 38.5); southern Africa (-24.0, 29.0)
- scholars split? Yes, on the route (through the rainforest early, or around it and down the east) and on the exact dates, not on the origin or on the fact of a large migration.

Claims:

1. Bantu languages began near the border of today's Nigeria and Cameroon, 4,000-5,000 years ago.
   - [Koile 2022]: the homeland "is generally believed to be near the border of Nigeria and Cameroon," with an origin between 4,000 and 5,000 years before present; their own model puts the root at a median of 5,110 BP and again "the region of the Guinea Gulf (around the border between current Nigeria and Cameroon)."
   - [de Filippo 2012]: "it is well accepted that Bantu languages spread from their homeland (Cameroon/Nigeria) approximately 5000 years ago."
   - [Grollemund 2015]: the expansion "swept out of West Central Africa beginning ~5,000 y ago."
2. The expansion was a movement of people, not just words, and it reached east and south over thousands of years.
   - [Fortes-Lima 2024]: "There is a broad interdisciplinary consensus that the initial spread of Bantu languages was a demic expansion"; genetic diversity falls with distance from western Africa; Bantu speakers took in significant ancestry from local groups they met.
   - [de Filippo 2012]: "evidence for a demic diffusion in the genetic data."
   - [Skoglund 2017]: the spread of farmers from western Africa involved complete replacement of local hunter-gatherers in some regions.
3. How the migrants got past the central African rainforest is disputed.
   - [Grollemund 2015]: groups "followed emerging savannah corridors," with the rainforest slowing them (by about 300 years on entry).
   - [Koile 2022]: their model "supports the hypothesis of an expansion through Central African tropical forests at 4,420 BP," earlier than a savanna corridor opened.
   - [de Filippo 2012]: contrasts an "early-split" model (Eastern languages north of the forest toward the Great Lakes about 4,000 years ago) with a "late-split" model (Eastern languages diverging south of the forest about 2,000 years ago), and finds "little support for the early-split model" in their data. Note [Koile 2022] reach a different, earlier-through-forest answer, so the studies do not agree.
4. Bantu is a solid, well-supported family even though "Niger-Congo" as a whole is debated.
   - [Glottolog]: Narrow Bantu is a family node of its own inside Atlantic-Congo (about 560 languages).
   - [Fortes-Lima 2024]: more than 500 languages, about 350 million speakers.
5. The far south was reached much later than the start. WEAK: I could only confirm "about 1.7 thousand years ago for the east of southern Africa" from the preprint version of [Schlebusch 2017] through a search summary. The published Science paper's wording was not read. Use "within the last two thousand years" or drop it.

Draft card text: "About 5,000 years ago, people speaking an early Bantu language began moving out from the Nigeria-Cameroon borderland. Over several thousand years their descendants spread east and south across Africa, and today more than 500 Bantu languages, including Swahili, Zulu and Shona, trace back to them."

## Swahili

- id: `swahili`
- kind: language
- date range: "from about 1,300 years ago; Arabic words arrive through trade over centuries"
- era year: 800
- location: the East African coast and islands (-6.0, 39.2), radius ~600 km: Mombasa to Kilwa including Zanzibar
- location uncertainty: Moderate. The coast is firmly the Swahili home. The exact cradle of the earliest form is not located here, and Glottolog's own point for the language sits inland (-8.3, 37.6), so use a coastal marker and say "coast".
- spread to (approximate, as a lingua franca): inland Tanzania and Kenya (-3.5, 35.0); the Great Lakes (-1.5, 31.0); eastern DR Congo (-3.0, 28.5)
- scholars split? Mildly, on how much Arabic vocabulary came when. Everyone agrees the core is Bantu.

Claims:

1. Swahili is a Bantu language of the East African coast.
   - [Brielle 2023]: "Kiswahili is an African Bantu language with Asian loanwords."
   - [Glottolog]: Swahili sits inside Bantu's East Bantu, Northeast Coastal group (Sabaki subgroup).
2. Its history is usually dated in the first millennium AD, with the recognisable Swahili language from about the 8th century.
   - [WOLD Swahili]: periodisation following Nurse and Hinnebusch 1993: proto-North-East-Coast 1-500 CE, proto-Sabaki 500-700, proto-Swahili 700-800, "pre-modern Swahili" 800-1940.
   - [Pouwels 1999] calls Nurse and Hinnebusch 1993 "the most comprehensive study yet done of Swahili history through linguistic analysis."
3. Arabic is its largest non-Bantu source of words, from centuries of trade around the Indian Ocean.
   - [Brielle 2023]: Arabic loanwords are "the single largest non-Bantu element in Kiswahili (16-20% of words)," Persian words up to 3%, reflecting "a millennium and a half of intense interaction with societies around the Indian Ocean rim."
   - [Brielle 2023] (DNA): ancient DNA from 80 people in medieval coastal towns shows African and Asian (mostly Persian) ancestry mixing from about AD 1000, alongside the spread of Islam.
4. Today Swahili is a lingua franca across East and Central Africa.
   - [UNESCO Kiswahili]: Kiswahili connects "more than 200 million speakers across East, Central and Southern Africa"; 7 July is World Kiswahili Language Day (the first African language honoured this way by the UN system). The 200 million figure is a UNESCO-level estimate and is at the high end; first-language speakers are far fewer. Treat as "tens of millions of first and second language speakers, and over 200 million by UNESCO's count" or use a hedge.

Disagreement and what I dropped:

- Timing of the Arabic influence. [Brielle 2023] say Arabic loanwords "may be primarily due to incorporations after AD 1500." Older and trade-based accounts put contact much earlier, but I could not verify those through an opened source. A Kenyan journal article by Chimerah ("Mwanga wa Lugha") argues Arabic words are very recent; its language and method are weak. WEAK, dropped.
- I could not open Ethnologue or Britannica (both returned 403), so speaker counts rest on UNESCO only.

Draft card text: "Swahili is a Bantu language from the East African coast. Centuries of Indian Ocean trade filled it with Arabic words, and it grew into a language people use to talk across many countries in East and Central Africa."

## Nilo-Saharan (a contested grouping)

- id: `nilo-saharan`
- kind: family (flag in the UI as "proposed")
- date range: "unknown"
- era year: null
- location: (12.0, 25.0), radius ~2000 km: the Sahel and upper Nile, central Sudan and Chad. Outlying groups: Songhay in Mali (16.7, -0.4); Nilotic in East Africa (3.0, 33.5).
- location uncertainty: High. Speakers are scattered in pockets from Mali to Tanzania, and a homeland is unproven.
- spread to: none; do not draw arrows from a family whose unity is unproven. A "Nilotic" spread can be shown only as its own family.
- scholars split? Yes. Whether Greenberg's Nilo-Saharan is one family at all: some specialists accept a core group; others say the evidence is not enough, and Glottolog does not use the label.

Claims:

1. "Nilo-Saharan" is Greenberg's 1963 grouping of many languages scattered from Mali to northern Tanzania. Many specialists see it as unproven.
   - [Guldemann 2018] (via the author's own abstract): Greenberg's four-family map of Africa has been reassessed; he concludes only two big families are firmly supported (Afroasiatic and Niger-Congo, each in a narrower sense) and that Nilo-Saharan and Khoisan should "for the time being" be split into smaller units. The "two families" wording reached me through a lecture summary found by search: WEAK on exact wording; solid that the chapter argues for splitting.
   - [Campbell 2008], as quoted by Guldemann: Greenberg's classification is "badly in need of major reinvestigation and reworking."
2. Glottolog does not use "Nilo-Saharan" as a family, and lists its parts separately.
   - [Glottolog]: its top-level African families include Central Sudanic (63 languages), Nilotic (56), Saharan (10), Maban (10), Kuliak (3), Koman (5) and Songhay as separate groupings, with no Nilo-Saharan node (checked in the language tree).
3. Specialists who defend the grouping say a core of languages shares stable grammar words, but also that the edges are uncertain.

## Khoisan and click languages

- id: `khoisan-click-languages`
- kind: family is not correct. Use "language area". Closest schema value: family, flagged "label, not a family".
- date range: "the languages have no agreed date; the people have some of the oldest known branches in our family tree (100,000+ years)"
- era year: null
- location: the Kalahari basin of Namibia and Botswana (-22.0, 21.0), radius ~900 km. Second area: northern Tanzania (-4.0, 35.0) for Hadza and Sandawe.
- location uncertainty: Moderate for today's languages (Glottolog points sit in the Kalahari and its edges); high for where speakers lived long ago.
- spread to: none
- scholars split? Not much on the main point. Specialists mostly agree "Khoisan" is not a proven family. They differ on whether some of the pieces are related to each other.

Claims:

1. "Khoisan" is a label for click languages of southern and eastern Africa that share clicks but are not shown to be one family.
   - [Guldemann 2014a]: "'Khoisan' in a linguistic sense can be viewed, at best, as a negative entity. It comprises a diverse range of languages in southern and eastern Africa which share the typological feature of phonemic clicks, yet cannot all be related genealogically to each other or any established lineage."
   - [Guldemann 2014b] (volume abstract): Greenberg's family idea "has been harshly criticized by linguists working on these languages"; the evidence "has proved to be seriously insufficient."
2. The southern African click languages fall into three separate families: Kx'a, Tuu and Khoe-Kwadi.
   - [Guldemann 2014b]: the volume presents data from "all three relevant lineages, Tuu, Kx'a and Khoe-Kwadi," and supports the idea that Greenberg's "Southern African Khoisan" is "better explained in terms of complex linguistic, cultural and genetic convergence" (contact in the Kalahari Basin).
   - [Glottolog]: Khoe-Kwadi (14 languages), Tuu (9) and Kxa (5) are separate families; Hadza and Sandawe (northern Tanzania) are each treated as isolates.
3. Clicks are not unique to one family. Two other African families have click languages through contact, and two East African isolates have clicks.
   - [Guldemann 2014a]: other languages "with a similar areal and typological profile" belong to Bantu (Niger-Congo) and Cushitic (Afroasiatic).
   - [Pickrell 2012]: the East African Hadza and Sandawe speak non-Bantu click languages and "derive a fraction of their ancestry from admixture with a population related to the Khoisan," supporting an ancient link between southern and eastern Africa (a genetic point, not a language-family point).
4. The people who speak these languages are genetically very old branches of our species, but that is about ancestry, not language age.
   - [Schlebusch 2012]: Khoe-San groups "diverged from other populations ≥100,000 years ago."
   - [Schlebusch 2017]: the first split among modern human populations estimated at 350,000 to 260,000 years ago.
   - [Skoglund 2017]: ancestry related to today's San was once more widespread, forming about two-thirds of the ancestry of Malawi hunter-gatherers (8,100-2,500 years ago) and about one-third of Tanzanian hunter-gatherers 1,400 years ago.
   - Do not turn "old lineage" into "oldest language": no source supports a language age.
5. Bantu farmers and herders took over much of the old click-speaking range.
   - [Skoglund 2017]: the spread of farmers from western Africa replaced local hunter-gatherers entirely in some regions.
   - [Pickrell 2012]: all southern Khoisan individuals derive at least a few percent of their genomes from non-Khoisan admixture starting ~1,200 years ago.

Draft card text: "'Khoisan' is a name for click languages in southern and eastern Africa. Linguists now see at least three separate families there, plus two lone languages in Tanzania, that picked up their clicks and other traits from living side by side. The name describes a region and a sound, not one family."
